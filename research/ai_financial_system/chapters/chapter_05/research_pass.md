# Chapter 5 Research Pass

## Provisional title

**Power Before Compute — Generation, Transmission, Special Contracts, and Stranded-Cost Risk**

## Research question

How does a proposed AI load become an approved, funded, interconnected, and energized
electrical service obligation; which party pays for generation, transmission, storage,
fuel, and interconnection; and who bears the loss when load arrives late, consumes less
than planned, defaults, or disappears?

## Boundary from Chapters 2–4

Chapter 2 mapped entity and counterparty capital. Chapter 3 separated announced compute
from contracted, delivered, and utilized capacity. Chapter 4 reconstructed project and
asset finance through commissioning and customer acceptance. Chapter 5 follows the
parallel electrical system.

It will not:

- equate announced load with utility-obligated service;
- equate regulatory approval with construction, commercial operation, or energization;
- equate nameplate generation with deliverable data-center capacity;
- equate energy supply with planning-reserve adequacy;
- equate a customer's contribution with utility ownership;
- equate a minimum bill with complete cost recovery;
- treat a parent guaranty or letter of credit as unlimited;
- treat renewable certificates, PPAs, and physical hourly supply as interchangeable; or
- describe a cost as “ratepayer funded” without tracing tariff and regulatory allocation.

## Required ledgers

1. **Load status** — announced, studied, contracted, approved, interconnected,
   energized, peak demand, minimum billing demand, and actual consumption.
2. **Physical assets** — generation, storage, substations, transmission, distribution,
   interconnection, fuel supply, owner, nameplate, accredited capacity, COD, and status.
3. **Capital stack** — utility rate base, customer contribution, project debt, tax
   credit, public grant, vendor finance, fuel contract, and unrecovered balance.
4. **Special contracts and tariffs** — demand charge, energy charge, minimum bill,
   ramp, delay, termination, collateral, guaranty, curtailment, and renewal.
5. **Cost allocation** — customer, guarantor, letter-of-credit bank, utility equity,
   other ratepayer, taxpayer, market participant, and asset-sale recovery.
6. **Grid dependency** — interconnection queue, transmission upgrade, reserve margin,
   capacity accreditation, seasonal adequacy, fuel, environmental permit, and outage.
7. **Accounting and control** — utility ownership, customer-supplied asset, VIE or
   project ownership, rate-base recognition, regulatory asset or liability, and
   off-balance-sheet power purchase.
8. **Failure waterfall** — delayed load, cancelled campus, fuel-price shock, generation
   delay, transmission delay, customer default, guarantor default, curtailment, and
   stranded asset.

- Entergy's initial Meta plan contemplated three combined-cycle units totaling 2,260 MW,

- A March 2026 Meta–Entergy announcement expanded the described portfolio to seven gas

- SpaceX/xAI disclosed $805 million of turbine purchase commitments through 2029 and an

- Amazon disclosed derivative-accounted energy contracts covering approximately
  200 million MWh with about fifteen years weighted remaining duration and most delivery

These are starting observations, not reconciled electrical capital stacks.

## Pass 1A — Meta–Entergy: “full cost” with a post-contract remainder

The Richland Parish record is not one power project. It is at least two Meta customer
SPVs, multiple service agreements, several generations of resource plan, and three
different transmission-funding methods. The original Laidley project and later Evest
expansion share a Meta parent guarantor and portions of a common grid, but they cannot
be treated as one customer contract.

### The resource scopes are versions, not additive capacity

Entergy's December 2024 announcement described:

- three combined-cycle units totaling 2,260 MW, two in Richland Parish;
- two Entergy-owned and six customer-owned substations;
- nearly 100 miles of 500-kV transmission; and
- eight new 230-kV lines.

The generators were then expected online during 2028–2029. By December 2025, the
Commission had approved two Richland plants totaling approximately 1,500 MW, construction
had begun, and both were targeted for late 2028. The public record reviewed here does not
say whether the third original plant was cancelled, moved, deferred, or absorbed into a
later plan.

In March 2026, a new announcement associated with an expansion that could take the Meta
campus to 5 GW described:

- seven combined-cycle plants exceeding 5,200 MW;
- about 240 miles of new 500-kV transmission;
- storage at three sites;
- nuclear uprates;
- support for up to 2,500 MW of solar; and
- a memorandum to explore future nuclear supply.

The correct ledger treatment is versioned overlap. The 2,260 MW original plan, 1,500 MW
approved subset, and greater-than-5,200 MW expanded portfolio are not 8,960 MW of
generation. Plant names, dockets, site locations, and later approvals must resolve which
assets supersede or expand prior scope.

### Evest requires generation and transmission

Entergy's public testimony says the existing system cannot serve Evest even after
Laidley-related investment. A transmission-only solution is also insufficient: Evest's
load requires new generation capacity and energy for physical service and planning
reserves. That distinction matters. A new line can move electricity, but it does not
create the accredited capacity or energy required to serve a multi-gigawatt load.

The Evest filing partitions transmission into three projects:

| Project | Physical scope | Funding route |
|---|---|---|
| Customer interconnection | West Fork Creek 500/230-kV substation, 20–30 miles of 230-kV lines, nine auxiliary switching stations | direct customer CIAC |
| Smalling–El Dorado | approximately 90 miles of 500-kV line split between Louisiana and Arkansas owners | direct customer CIAC with both utilities |
| West Fork Creek–St. Landry | system 500-kV line and switching station | utility investment recovered through Evest ESA revenue |

The distinction changes ownership and residual risk. CIAC cash directly funds assets
that Entergy constructs and owns. The system line instead enters the utility investment
perimeter and is recovered through tariff revenue during the service agreement.

### The customer bears estimate risk during development

The public cost exhibit is sealed. The estimates are Class 5—high-level and preliminary—
and Evest changed its site layout during development. Entergy says the customer elected
to proceed on that basis to meet its schedule. Cost uncertainty on the interconnection
and Smalling–El Dorado projects therefore falls to the customer through CIAC. The system
line uses an ESA true-up designed to keep estimate changes from Entergy or other customers
during the agreement term.

The dated public schedule is unusually specific:

| Asset | Construction target | In-service target |
|---|---|---|
| Evest customer interconnection | Sep. 2026–Aug. 2030 | 1 Aug. 2030 |
| St. Landry station | Nov. 2027–Aug. 2029 | 1 Dec. 2031 |
| Smalling–El Dorado | Mar. 2028–Dec. 2030 | 30 Dec. 2030 |
| West Fork Creek–St. Landry | Jun. 2028–Dec. 2031 | 1 Dec. 2031 |

The mismatch between physical completion and stated in-service dates must remain
visible. A station can finish construction before the line or network configuration
needed for service.

### “One hundred percent during the term” is not “zero forever”

Entergy expects Evest revenue to offset 100% of proposed transmission revenue
requirements during the original ESA term. If Evest terminates early, a termination fee
mitigates remaining investment. Future upgrades identified after operating data becomes
available are solely the customer's responsibility.

Yet the same testimony says that if the system line is not ultimately converted to CIAC,
other utility customers will bear the final 16% of estimated project cost on a
net-present-value basis after the original term. At that point, approximately 54% of the
asset's assumed 36-year physical life remains after about sixteen years of depreciation.

Those percentages answer different questions:

- 100% is expected revenue-requirement recovery during the ESA;
- 54% is remaining physical useful life; and
- 16% is other customers' final share of estimated cost on an NPV basis.

The advertised “full cost of service” is therefore term-bounded. It can coexist with a
post-contract rate-base remainder and shared system value. That is not necessarily a
subsidy: the line provides regional reliability benefits and may defer other upgrades.
It is a documented transfer of terminal asset risk that must be valued rather than
described away.

### Load loss is a system contingency

The most unexpected result is that load disappearance can stress the grid as well as
load growth. Entergy modeled a 2035 fault causing Laidley and Evest to disconnect
together. Without the West Fork Creek–St. Landry line, a separate 500-kV element reached
123% loading in summer and 137% in winter. With the line, those modeled levels fell to
103% and 121%.

The new transmission is thus not only an import path into data centers. It also mitigates
the network shock created if several gigawatts vanish abruptly. Common-parent projects
create correlated load-trip risk. The line has grid value even in a scenario where the
AI load itself disappears from the electrical system for a fault.

### Reconstructed cost waterfall

1. Evest or Meta support supplies CIAC cash for direct interconnection assets.
2. Evest ESA revenue recovers system-line revenue requirements during the original term.
3. True-ups allocate Class-5 estimate changes back to the customer.
4. Later operationally required upgrades remain the customer's responsibility.
5. Early termination invokes the redacted termination-fee formula and parent support.
6. After the original term, other customers can bear the documented 16% NPV remainder.
7. Shared reliability and avoided-upgrade value offset—but do not contractually erase—
   that remaining exposure.

## First contradiction and opacity queue

1. Meta's original 2,260 MW plan, two approved 1,500 MW plants, and later greater-than-
   5,200 MW seven-plant portfolio may represent supersession, expansion, or different
   project scopes; they cannot be added without plant-level reconciliation.
2. “Meta pays full cost” must be traced through contributions, tariff charges, guaranties,
   fuel clauses, plant utilization, benefit sharing, and residual utility exposure.
3. Saline's “existing resources plus a battery” description conflicts with sworn evidence
   that existing resources were insufficient in every season for reserve adequacy.
4. Nameplate storage MW do not establish MWh duration, accredited seasonal capacity,
   construction cost, or commercial-operation status.
5. xAI turbine purchase commitments, acquired turbine assets, leased equipment, and
   operating units may overlap; permit status and physical operation must be dated per unit.
6. Alphabet guarantee maxima, acquired Intersect assets, PPAs, and energy-development
   commitments overlap across consolidated and unconsolidated perimeters.
7. Amazon's 200 million MWh is an aggregate derivative notional over time, not annual
   data-center consumption or proven new generation.
8. Large-load contract protections can still fail if the customer, parent guarantor,
   collateral bank, and recoverable asset value deteriorate together.

## Pass 1 writing gate

Chapter 5 cannot enter writing until:

- at least five power systems have asset, contract, and cost-allocation reconstructions;
- load MW, generation nameplate, accredited capacity, storage MW/MWh, and energy MWh
  remain separate measurement classes;
- at least four systems have dated approval-to-construction-to-COD-to-energization ladders;
- customer contributions, utility rate base, project debt, public support, tax credits,
  and guarantees are reconciled without addition across overlapping perimeters;
- minimum bills, delay rights, termination formulas, collateral, guaranties, and
  curtailment are mapped for each Tier 1 contract where public;
- utility consolidation and third-party project ownership are documented;
- ratepayer, utility-equity, taxpayer, customer, guarantor, and asset-recovery layers
  are separately assigned;
- a stranded-cost and power-failure waterfall is frozen; and
- all material unknowns name a docket, tariff, interconnection study, permit, financing
  agreement, or project filing that could resolve them.

## Pass 1B — xAI / SpaceX Memphis power reconstruction

### Evidence boundary

The reconstruction uses two municipal-utility status statements and SpaceX's June 2026
prospectus. The utility statements are point-in-time evidence; the prospectus is later
company disclosure. They are not contradictory merely because the power system changed.
The credible reconstruction must therefore preserve dates rather than selecting one
capacity headline.

At Lowry Road, MLGW reported 150 MW of grid service: 8 MW from an existing substation and
142 MW from a newly constructed substation. A second 150-MW request required TVA approval,
customer-funded transmission upgrades and another substation. The customer agreed to
demand response and funded the relevant delivery infrastructure without MLGW capital.
MLGW also described more than 50 MW of utility-scale batteries coordinated with the
system. These figures establish delivered grid capacity, requested incremental capacity,
and a storage-power lower bound. They do not establish synchronized compute consumption,
storage energy duration, or continuous grid draw.

At Tulane Road on July 22 2025, MLGW stated that the site received only approximately
0.5 MW of general service and that MLGW was not powering the Colossus II supercomputer.
SpaceX later described Colossus II as capable of operating entirely from a self-built
behind-the-meter gigawatt-scale gas plant with batteries and later grid integration.
The appropriate inference is a staged hybrid architecture: minimal municipal service at
the earlier date, followed by large customer-controlled self-generation. It is not valid
to treat the 0.5-MW reading and the later one-gigawatt capability as contemporaneous
alternative descriptions of the same service.

### Capacity classes that must not be added

1. Lowry's 150 MW is documented grid delivery.
2. The second 150 MW is requested incremental grid service, not proven current delivery.
3. More than 50 MW is storage power, not storage energy or firm generation.
4. Approximately 1 GW is company-reported behind-the-meter generation capability.
5. SpaceX's approximately 1 GW of aggregate AI-compute power describes compute
   infrastructure and is not automatically the turbine plant's independently verified
   dispatched output.
6. The $805 million purchase commitment and separate approximately $2 billion acquisition
   agreement are monetary obligations, not megawatts, and may overlap with later installed
   inventory.

### Reconstructed funding and risk waterfall

1. The customer funds dedicated Lowry delivery upgrades and substations.
2. Grid consumption produces prevailing industrial-tariff revenue for MLGW and TVA.
3. Demand response transfers peak-system reliability protection to curtailment risk for
   the compute operator.
4. Batteries buffer grid peaks and behind-the-meter power quality, but ownership,
   duration and dispatch priority remain undisclosed.
5. SpaceX group capital funds owned or leased turbine assets, including $805 million of
   purchase commitments through 2029.
6. A separate approximately $2 billion turbine acquisition agreed April 30 2026 was
   subject to closing; it is not recorded as funded or closed.
7. Self-generation transfers fuel, maintenance, emissions, availability and residual-
   value risk from the utility delivery system to the AI sponsor.
8. Environmental litigation can impose operating limits, retrofit costs or penalties;
   the prospectus reports allegations rather than a final liability.
9. Ultimately, site-level compute revenue must recover turbine, fuel, storage,
   accelerator and financing costs. No complete site cash waterfall is public.

### Control and dependency result

Customer funding can protect utility capital without making the power system low risk.
Lowry leaves delivery and bulk-system control with MLGW and TVA while granting the
customer a large interruptible load and storage interface. Colossus II internalizes more
generation control but also internalizes fuel logistics, equipment uptime, permitting and
stranded-turbine risk. The migration from grid service toward self-generation changes the
identity of the risk holder; it does not eliminate the risk.

The strongest system-level finding is temporal. A data-center campus can move through
general service, dedicated grid infrastructure, hybrid operation and mostly
behind-the-meter generation. Any database that stores a single undated “power capacity”
will produce false contradictions and double counts. Chapter 5 therefore requires a
dated power-source ledger for each campus.

## Pass 1C — Oracle / DTE Saline: contractual defense is not physical readiness

Saline supplies the clearest public large-load contract in the sample. Green Chile
Ventures—an Oracle subsidiary rather than OpenAI or the campus owner—is DTE's legal
customer. Its Primary Supply Agreement ramps to 1,383 MW, applies an 80% minimum billing
demand, and runs through February 2045 before two optional ten-year renewals. Green Chile
can delay the aggregate ramp by twelve months but cannot reduce the maximum contracted
load. At the completed ramp, the formula therefore produces a 1,106.4-MW billing floor.
That is revenue protection, not evidence of continuous consumption.

The Energy Storage Agreement creates a second asset and payment perimeter. DTE may
develop, own or procure up to 1,383 MW of storage. Green Chile pays each project's full
fifteen-year revenue requirement: construction financing, depreciation, return,
operations, tax and insurance. Incremental net MISO capacity, energy and ancillary-
service proceeds offset the customer invoice after applicable administration. Green
Chile cannot voluntarily terminate this agreement; after customer default, the
unrecovered project revenue requirement becomes due.

Only 332 MW of this storage program had documented project approvals by March 27 2026.
The 1,383-MW figure is therefore a planned contractual ceiling, while 332 MW is approved
capacity. Neither is proven operating capacity. Storage MWh, duration, accredited
seasonal value, construction cost and commercial-operation dates remain separate gaps.

### Saline loss waterfall

1. Green Chile pays monthly D11 demand, energy and surcharge amounts.
2. The 80% minimum billing demand supports DTE during under-utilization.
3. Early PSA termination invokes at least the greater of twenty-four months of minimum
   charges or charges through the tenth anniversary of ramp completion.
4. Each storage project receives its full fifteen-year revenue requirement, net of
   specified wholesale-market credits.
5. Green Chile default invokes the remaining storage revenue requirement.
6. Oracle parent support and a rating-triggered letter of credit back Green Chile.
7. If customer, parent and collateral recoveries are insufficient, the Commission
   assigns the residual to DTE rather than other customers.
8. Operationally, the data center is shed before other firm customers when that can
   avoid broader emergency curtailment.

The last two provisions reveal a deliberate split. Ratepayers receive financial and
reliability protection; DTE equity retains a concentrated counterparty tail; the data
center accepts lower emergency priority. A customer-financed utility asset is therefore
not risk-free to the utility. It is a contractually defended exposure with a named final
loss bearer.

## Pass 1D — Alphabet / Intersect: acquisition perimeter versus power perimeter

Alphabet's March 2026 Intersect acquisition did not transfer the entire legacy Intersect
fleet. The transaction announcement explicitly excluded existing operating assets in
Texas and operating and in-development assets in California. At closing, those assets
moved into independently owned IPX Power, supported by the pre-existing investor group.
IPX reported 4.4 GW of solar PV and 8.8 GWh of battery storage in construction or
operation. Those capacities sit outside Alphabet consolidation.

What entered Alphabet was a different perimeter: the team and multiple gigawatts of
co-located energy and data-center projects in development or construction. Alphabet's
purchase accounting recorded $5.111 billion of property and equipment and $1.214 billion
of assumed debt, but did not translate those balances into project-specific MW, MWh,
completion percentages or offtakes.

Meitner exposes the delivery sequence. Intersect describes more than one gigawatt of
wind, solar and battery systems plus a minority share of on-site firming gas. The Quantum
Clean Energy Project began operation in June 2026 while the Google data center had only
recently begun construction. Thus:

- acquired asset value is not capacity;
- mixed-resource MW are not firm MW;
- storage MW and MWh are not disclosed;
- operating power does not prove accepted compute capacity; and
- excluded IPX capacity cannot be added to acquired Intersect capacity.

Alphabet also retains a second, external power perimeter. Its energy service agreements
run two to twenty years, generally include take-or-pay minimums and substantive
termination fees, and sit within a larger contractual-obligation total. Separately,
Alphabet reports $9.0 billion of maximum potential payments under guarantees supporting
long-lead equipment for external power projects; after a guarantee payment it may receive
assets. The maximum is contingent exposure, not funded capex, expected loss or power
capacity.

Alphabet consequently operates three distinct control modes at once:

1. consolidated ownership through acquired Intersect;
2. contract control through long-duration energy purchases; and
3. contingent support of external VIE equipment through financial guarantees.

The same company can own one project's development platform, buy another project's
output and backstop a third project's equipment without consolidating the latter two.

## Pass 1E — Amazon: a 200-million-MWh portfolio is a duration distribution

Amazon's energy disclosure is unusually large but deliberately aggregated. Approximately
200 million MWh were subject to derivative accounting at March 31 2026, with a weighted
average remaining duration of approximately fifteen years and most delivery beyond nine
years. Dividing the aggregate quantity by the weighted duration gives a scale equivalent
of 13.3 million MWh per year, or approximately 1.52 GW of continuous average power if
deliveries were uniform. They are not: Amazon explicitly says the portfolio is
back-loaded.

The derivative classification reveals contract behavior. When operational variability
causes consumption to differ from committed quantities, Amazon may make or receive net
cash payments rather than take physical delivery. The contracts are not trading
positions, but their settlement mechanics create financial exposure. Long-dated fair
value uses Level 3 inputs beyond the four-to-six-year portion of the market with more
liquid data. Remeasurement can affect operating expense and the balance sheet without
current cash flow.

Exit economics are separate again. Amazon generally can terminate by paying fixed
penalties such as reimbursing the counterparty for new-construction costs. Those penalties
are generally not the derivative fair value. The power system therefore has at least
three measurements:

- remaining physical or net-settled MWh;
- accounting fair value under modeled long-dated prices, volatility and credit; and
- contractual exit cost tied in part to project construction.

None can substitute for the other. Without contract-level projects and delivery shapes,
the 200-million-MWh number is evidence of duration and scale—not annual consumption,
owned generation, incremental grid load or a single stranded-cost exposure.
