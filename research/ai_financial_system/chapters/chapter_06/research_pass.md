# Chapter 6 Research Pass

## Provisional title

**Frontier Laboratories — OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, and the Economics Hidden by Private
Status**

## Pass 1 objective

Reconstruct each frontier laboratory as a legal, funding, compute, revenue, ownership,
and control system. Do not estimate enterprise value or “burn” from press narratives.
Trace what public counterparties, official announcements, debt records, project
documents, and filed financial statements actually establish.

The chapter must answer:

1. Which entity raises capital, issues securities, signs compute contracts, employs
   researchers, owns models, and receives customer revenue?
2. Which capital is funded cash, undrawn commitment, contingent facility, strategic
   investment, cloud credit, secondary liquidity, or non-cash capacity?
3. How much supplier/customer circularity exists without assuming that every strategic
   investment is a subsidy?
4. Which compute commitments are unconditional, usage-based, milestone-gated,
   terminable, resale-reducible, or financed by the same counterparty providing cloud?
5. What accounting evidence appears on public counterparties' books even when the
   laboratory itself is private?
6. Who controls governance, model commercialization, infrastructure selection,
   exclusivity, safety decisions, liquidity events, and default remedies?
7. Which revenue is external customer demand versus strategic-counterparty or
   related-party flow?
8. Where can a laboratory's failure transmit into hyperscaler capex, developer rent,
   GPU utilization, private credit, vendor guarantees, or public markets?

## Measurement rules

- Never add a facility ceiling to an equity investment unless funded status and legal
  consideration differ.
- Separate primary capital from secondary share purchases.
- Separate security fair value on an investor's books from cash originally invested.
- Separate cloud commitment, recognized cloud revenue, delivered capacity and customer
  utilization.
- Separate contracted capacity from project cost and from the laboratory's own capex.
- Separate a parent guarantee from the guaranteed obligation and from expected loss.
- Separate a common-control combination from an arm's-length cash acquisition.
- Separate consolidated SpaceX AI-segment figures from stand-alone xAI economics unless
  the filing supplies a bridge.
- Label counterparty-supplied compute as purchased input even when the counterparty also
  owns laboratory securities.

## Contradiction and opacity queue

1. OpenAI capitalization announcements often combine primary investment, conditional
   commitments, secondary liquidity and compute arrangements.
2. Amazon's OpenAI investment and AWS arrangement are economically connected but not
   disclosed as a dollar-for-dollar recycling mechanism.
3. Anthropic's Amazon facility, investment option and cloud milestones overlap; maximum
   headlines cannot be added.
4. Alphabet anonymizes the laboratory attached to its $10 billion plus $30 billion
   milestone structure in the filing.
5. Data-center project value is repeatedly described as model-laboratory “spend” even

6. SpaceX's combined AI segment can obscure stand-alone xAI revenue, cost and capital

7. Model-company revenue estimates in reporting may use bookings, run rate, annualized
   monthly revenue, contracted commitments or recognized revenue interchangeably.
8. Strategic investors can be suppliers and customers without every dollar returning

## Pass 1A — OpenAI control and current operating perimeter

### Control is not proportional to economic ownership

The October 2025 recapitalization placed conventional economic ownership and governing
control on different axes. OpenAI Foundation held 26% of OpenAI Group PBC and a
valuation-linked warrant. Microsoft held approximately 27% on an as-converted diluted
basis; employees and other investors held the remaining 47%. Yet the Foundation alone
holds special voting and governance rights to appoint every Group director and replace
directors at any time.

This creates three distinct measurements:

- economic ownership and exposure to Group value;
- formal board control through Foundation rights; and
- contractual control over IP distribution compute supply and commercial payments.

Microsoft's approximate 27% economic stake does not establish board control. Conversely,
the Foundation's 26% economic stake understates its formal governance authority.

### The Microsoft relationship changed twice

The October 2025 definitive agreement preserved Microsoft's exclusive model and product
IP rights and Azure API exclusivity subject to the disclosed AGI framework. It extended
specified IP rights through 2032, removed Microsoft's compute-provider right of first
refusal, and added an incremental $250 billion Azure-services purchase.

The April 2026 amendment changed the boundary again:

1. Microsoft remained OpenAI's primary cloud partner and received first product
   shipment unless it could not or chose not to support required capabilities.
2. Microsoft's model and product IP license became nonexclusive through 2032.
3. OpenAI could serve all products through other clouds.
4. Microsoft stopped paying revenue share to OpenAI.
5. OpenAI continued paying Microsoft the same undisclosed percentage through 2030,
   independent of technology progress, subject to an undisclosed total cap.

The public amendment did not restate or cancel the $250 billion Azure purchase. It is
therefore unsafe to assume either that the commitment disappeared or that its original
economics survived unchanged. The unredacted amended agreement is required.

### Current revenue evidence is a run-rate statement

OpenAI reported on March 31 2026:

- $2 billion of revenue per month;
- enterprise customers contributing more than 40% of revenue;
- more than 900 million weekly active users; and
- more than 50 million subscribers.

These observations establish commercial scale but not audited annual economics. Monthly
revenue cannot be multiplied by twelve and treated as recognized annual revenue without
seasonality and accounting evidence. Users are not customers; subscribers are not
necessarily full-price monthly subscribers; enterprise mix does not reveal customer
concentration or gross margin.

### Compute diversification is now explicit

OpenAI named Microsoft, Oracle, AWS, CoreWeave and Google Cloud as cloud pathways and
NVIDIA, AMD, AWS Trainium, Cerebras, Broadcom and its internal-chip project as silicon
pathways. This is not the elimination of infrastructure dependence. It is a portfolio of
dependencies with different contract terms, hardware compatibility, delivery dates and
capital providers.

The same investor can be a supplier:

- Microsoft supplies primary cloud and owns approximately 27%.
- Amazon supplies AWS and funded preferred equity.
- NVIDIA supplies the dominant accelerator base and anchored the funding round.
- SoftBank supplies equity and participates in infrastructure development.

No primary source reviewed establishes immediate dollar-for-dollar recycling from each
investment into that investor's products. Circularity must be tested at the contract and
cash-date level.

### Committed capital is not closing cash

OpenAI described its March 2026 financing as $122 billion of committed capital. Amazon's
filing separately shows $15 billion funded and approximately $35 billion remaining.
SoftBank uses scheduled tranches, and OpenAI's approximately $4.7 billion revolving
facility remained undrawn at closing. The headline therefore combines funded and future
liquidity. An instrument-level closing ledger is required before calculating cash runway.

## Pass 1B — Anthropic: financing availability depends on compute delivery

### The Series H total embeds strategic-investor components

Anthropic reported raising a $65 billion Series H at a $965 billion post-money
valuation. The same announcement says that $15 billion of previously committed
hyperscaler investments is included in the round, including $5 billion from Amazon.
The announcement does not supply an instrument-level closing cash schedule. The
capital ledger therefore records the aggregate as reported round capital, treats
Amazon's separately documented $5 billion as the minimum traceable funded amount, and
leaves the remaining cash timing unresolved. The correct relationship is:

`Series H aggregate = newly arranged components + embedded prior hyperscaler commitments`

It is not:

`Series H + Amazon $5B + other hyperscaler $10B`

Anthropic does not name the other $10 billion hyperscaler component in the primary
announcement. Alphabet's filing separately describes an anonymous $10 billion initial
security plus $30 billion of milestone-contingent future capital. High-quality reporting
identifies Anthropic, but the primary filing does not. The database therefore preserves
the link as high-confidence secondary identification rather than promoting it to a named
Grade A fact.

### Amazon occupies four sides of the same relationship

By April 2026, Amazon's Anthropic relationship contained four distinct exposures:

1. $8 billion invested in convertible notes from 2023 through 2025;
2. $5 billion of subsequent preferred equity;
3. a facility up to $20 billion whose availability starts at zero; and
4. Anthropic's commitment of more than $100 billion to AWS technologies over ten years
   for up to 5 GW of compute.

The financing facility is not ordinary committed liquidity. Amazon disclosed that
capacity becomes available as Amazon achieves compute-delivery milestones. Draws take
the form of new Anthropic convertible notes or, after a defined liquidity event, common
stock. Amazon also holds an option to invest up to $5 billion in future Anthropic
financings, and an exercised option reduces the facility amount.

This creates a contingent capital loop:

`Amazon delivers compute → Anthropic earns financing availability → Amazon funds new
Anthropic securities → Anthropic pays AWS for compute`

That is genuine circular structure, but not proof of same-day dollar recycling. The
delivery schedule, draw decisions, invoice dates and security issuances are not public.

### Compute diversification remains economically concentrated

Anthropic has announced:

- up to 5 GW from AWS and a greater-than-$100-billion ten-year technology commitment;
- 5 GW of next-generation Google and Broadcom TPU capacity;
- approximately 325,000 NVIDIA GPUs through SpaceX Colossus;
- $30 billion of Azure compute plus up to 1 GW of NVIDIA-powered Azure capacity; and
- access through Fluidstack and the River Bend development pathway.

These values do not share a unit or status. AWS's five gigawatts is a ceiling with dated
chip ramps. The Google/Broadcom total includes Broadcom's approximately 3.5-GW component.
Broadcom expressly says consumption depends on Anthropic's continued commercial success.
SpaceX's 325,000 GPUs carry a monthly price and termination rights. Azure's $30 billion
is a purchase commitment, while one gigawatt describes an additional capacity ceiling.

The portfolio reduces dependence on a single chip architecture while increasing
contract-management and delivery risk across several counterparties. AWS remains the
primary cloud and training partner and distributes Claude through Bedrock, so hardware
diversity does not remove platform concentration.

### Competitors can become each other's utilization backstop

SpaceX agreed to provide Anthropic approximately 325,000 GPUs at $1.25 billion per month
through May 2029 after lower ramp fees. Either party has disclosed exit rights after an
initial period and notice. A simple multiplication approaches a $45 billion ceiling, but
that is not fixed backlog.

The economic relationship is unusual: xAI and Anthropic compete at the model layer while
Anthropic supplies external utilization and revenue to the Colossus infrastructure.
SpaceX gains a customer for a massive fixed-asset base; Anthropic gains capacity outside
its hyperscaler pathways. Competitive failure at either laboratory can therefore affect
the other's capacity economics.

### Project obligations must stay with their legal obligors

River Bend illustrates another boundary. Hut 8 DC owns and finances the site. Fluidstack
owes the lease. Google guarantees specified tenant payments. Anthropic anchors downstream
demand and broader expansion. Public evidence does not make Anthropic the project-bond
issuer, direct landlord tenant or Google-guarantee beneficiary.

Describing River Bend as “Anthropic's $3.25 billion data-center debt” would transfer a
documented project obligation to the wrong entity. The laboratory's actual Fluidstack
service commitment remains private.

### Run-rate growth is not a cash-flow statement

Anthropic reported annualized run-rate revenue of approximately:

- $1 billion at the start of 2025;
- more than $5 billion in August 2025;
- $9 billion at year-end 2025;
- more than $30 billion in April 2026; and
- more than $47 billion in May 2026.

Those statements indicate exceptional current sales velocity, not audited annual
recognized revenue. The evidence package stores each dated observation as non-additive.
It does not infer gross margin, cash conversion, customer retention or the ability to
carry more than $130 billion of announced compute purchases from run rate alone.

## Pass 1C — xAI: the laboratory disappears inside a common-control perimeter

### Common-control accounting changes the question before it answers it

SpaceX's 2026 prospectus does not present xAI as a stand-alone reporting entity. It says
xAI acquired X on March 28, 2025 and SpaceX acquired xAI on February 2, 2026. Because
Elon Musk held majority voting interests before and after each transaction, the
combinations were accounted for as common-control transactions. The historical
statements were retrospectively recast at carrying value, intercompany balances were
eliminated, and no acquisition goodwill was recognized.

This is not merely a presentation detail. It prevents the public statements from
answering the most important laboratory-level questions. The reported “AI segment”
combines X's advertising, subscriptions and data licensing with Grok and infrastructure
activity. Consolidation eliminates some internal flows. Acquisition cash paid to
security holders is not operating liquidity delivered to xAI. A forensic reconstruction
must therefore preserve three perimeters:

1. stand-alone xAI, which is not publicly reported;
2. the combined X and xAI AI segment; and
3. the consolidated SpaceX parent after the February 2026 combination.

### The reported revenue is not xAI model revenue

The combined AI segment reported $3.201 billion of 2025 revenue: $1.844 billion of
advertising and $1.357 billion of “AI Solutions and Infrastructure,” a category that
includes X and Grok subscriptions plus data licensing. In the first quarter of 2026,
the segment reported $818 million of revenue.

These figures cannot be relabeled as xAI revenue. They do not disclose the split among
Grok subscriptions, enterprise or API usage, X Premium subscriptions, data licensing,
infrastructure services, or related-party demand. The database therefore records the
observations at the combined-segment perimeter and marks the missing product split as a
critical unknown.

### Capital intensity is observable; stand-alone burn is not

The combined AI segment reported:

- 2025 operating loss of $6.355 billion, adjusted EBITDA loss of $1.237 billion and
  capital expenditures of $12.727 billion; and
- first-quarter 2026 operating loss of $2.469 billion, adjusted EBITDA loss of
  $609 million and capital expenditures of $7.723 billion.

Capital expenditure equaled approximately 3.98 times 2025 revenue and 9.44 times
first-quarter 2026 revenue. These are capital-intensity ratios, not cash-burn measures.
The numerator can include assets used by X or shared infrastructure, while operating
cash flow, working capital, equipment finance and proceeds from asset transactions are
not supplied at the segment level.

The cost disclosures nevertheless identify real drivers. AI research and development
expense reached $5.064 billion in 2025; the increase included approximately $1.673
billion of GPU depreciation and $1.440 billion of infrastructure and cloud-computing
expense. This shows that both owned equipment and external cloud services sit inside the
cost base.

### The financing stack includes equity, secured debt and parent refinancing

xAI completed a $20 billion Series E in January 2026. A separate 2025 package provided
$5 billion through two term loans and 12.5% senior secured notes maturing in 2030. After
the SpaceX combination, a $20 billion unsecured bridge facility refinanced legacy X and
xAI debt and funded broader corporate purposes.

These values are not additive operating capital. The bridge refinances prior debt, and
its allocation between X, xAI and other purposes is not disclosed. The approximately
$2.947 billion of cash paid in the SpaceX-xAI common-control combination went to
security holders alongside a share exchange; it was not new laboratory cash.

### Related-party infrastructure creates both capacity and governance exposure

SpaceX discloses three compute-equipment arrangements with Valor-related entities whose
undiscounted payments total approximately $20.206 billion. Two intended sale-leasebacks
did not qualify for sale accounting and produced approximately $9.041 billion of
recognized debt at March 31, 2026. Valor's founder sits on SpaceX's board.

The correct ledger therefore separates:

`undiscounted contractual payments ≠ recognized debt ≠ equipment fair value`

Tesla represents a second related-party pathway. The combined group purchased
approximately $506 million of Megapacks and other products in 2025 and another
$303 million through April 2026. Tesla's conditional $2 billion xAI Series E commitment
converted after the merger into SpaceX Class A common stock issued in March 2026. The
equipment purchases are customer-to-supplier cash flows; the equity transaction is an
investor-to-parent financing flow. Combining them would obscure both.

### A competing laboratory may become Colossus's external utilization anchor

After the historical reporting period, SpaceX entered cloud-services agreements to
provide Anthropic approximately 325,000 GPUs for $1.25 billion per month, subject to ramp
pricing and termination rights. The arrangement could convert Colossus from a
single-laboratory asset into shared infrastructure. It could also transmit Anthropic
demand risk into the economics of xAI's asset base.

The agreement is prospective evidence, not support for 2025 or first-quarter 2026
revenue. Nor can monthly price multiplied by the maximum term be treated as contracted
backlog without modeling acceptance, ramp, exit and reallocation clauses.

### External cloud remains a hidden fixed commitment

SpaceX states that the combined group has noncancelable, multi-year third-party
cloud-computing commitments payable regardless of actual usage. The providers, values
and maturity schedule are not disclosed in the cited prospectus. xAI therefore combines
large owned or financed infrastructure with an unquantified fixed external-compute
obligation. Any runway estimate that looks only at Colossus capital expenditure is
incomplete.

## Pass 1D — Cross-laboratory reconstruction

### The three largest round headlines measure different things

OpenAI's $122 billion is a committed-capital aggregate with staged and conditional
funding. Anthropic's $65 billion Series H includes $15 billion of previously committed
hyperscaler investments and does not disclose an instrument-level cash schedule. xAI's
$20 billion Series E includes a delayed $2 billion Tesla component that ultimately
closed as SpaceX common stock after the common-control merger.

Ranking the laboratories by those headlines would therefore rank unlike measures. The
frozen capital-status bridge asks four different questions for each program:

1. What is the aggregate announced or committed amount?
2. What funded cash is independently traceable?
3. What remains undrawn, scheduled or conditional?
4. What component must not be added again?

That bridge produces a deliberately lower-confidence liquidity view than the press
release totals. It does not estimate runway because none of the three supplies a
comparable audited cash-flow statement.

### Supplier-investor overlap is not one uniform form of circularity

All three laboratories combine capital and infrastructure counterparties, but the
contractual mechanisms differ:

- OpenAI receives capital from major technology counterparties and buys compute across a
  broad provider portfolio. The reviewed public documents do not reveal a formula that
  makes funding availability depend directly on a provider's capacity delivery.
- Anthropic's Amazon facility does disclose that formula: financing availability grows
  as Amazon reaches compute-delivery milestones.
- xAI primarily financed an integrated asset base through equity, secured debt,
  parent refinancing and related-party equipment channels; it then added Anthropic as a
  prospective external customer.

“Circular financing” is therefore too coarse as a conclusion. Anthropic has a documented
supplier-performance-to-financing link. OpenAI has strategic overlap whose cash loop is
not publicly specified. xAI has asset, parent and related-party loops plus external
utilization risk.

### Control determines where losses can move

OpenAI separates economic ownership from nonprofit director control. Anthropic's public
announcements disclose large investors but not a complete voting, liquidation-preference
or reserved-matter map. xAI's financial perimeter moved under a common controller and
then into SpaceX, where historical figures are retrospectively combined and
intercompany flows disappear.

The same dollar of laboratory loss therefore has different governance consequences.
OpenAI investors can bear economic loss without controlling directors. Anthropic's
investor protections remain substantially private. xAI's shortfall can move through a
consolidated parent whose creditors and minority holders also finance non-AI assets.

### Risk leaves the laboratory through contracts before default

The ledgers identify at least five transmission channels that do not require a formal
laboratory insolvency:

1. fixed or contingent cloud purchases change supplier backlog and utilization;
2. project developers and lenders depend on downstream model demand;
3. supplier delivery can change the amount of customer financing available;
4. related-party equipment finance moves infrastructure value and refinancing risk
   across a controlled group; and
5. competitors can become each other's compute customer and capacity provider.

The public evidence establishes the pathways, not their common-shock probability or
loss-given-default. Quantifying those requires the private contracts, maturity schedules,
asset registers, cash collections and utilization telemetry listed in the unknowns
ledger.
