# Chapter 3 Research Pass

## Provisional title

**Compute Commitments — GPUs, Cloud Minimums, Prepayments, and Capacity Reservations**

## Research question

Who owns, finances, controls, and must pay for the compute capacity described in the
AI system, and how much of announced capacity is contracted, funded, delivered,
utilized, terminable, guaranteed, or contingent?

## Pass boundary

Chapter 2 established capital sources and entity-level risk pathways. Chapter 3 follows
the purchased or reserved compute unit itself. It will not treat a dollar financing
round as compute, a gigawatt as a contract value, a GPU count as title, RPO as delivered
capacity, or a cloud announcement as minimum spend.

## Required ledgers

1. **Compute capacity ledger** — product, quantity, power-equivalent capacity,
   delivery window, owner, operator, customer, and status.
2. **Commercial obligation ledger** — minimum spend, prepayment, reservation,
   cancellation, assignment, renewal, service credits, and remedies.
3. **Hardware title and finance ledger** — purchaser, owner, lender, lessor, collateral,
   residual value, and depreciation or financing treatment.
4. **Delivery and utilization ledger** — ordered, installed, energized, accepted,
   available, reserved, consumed, and idle capacity.
5. **Contradiction log** — competing GPU counts, capacity measures, contract values,
   terms, and legal-entity descriptions.

## Initial evidence inherited from Chapter 2

- CoreWeave's NVIDIA platform concentration, $6.3 billion residual-capacity order,
  OpenAI and Meta cloud orders, and $98.8 billion RPO.
- OpenAI's NVIDIA ten-gigawatt letter of intent, AMD one-to-six-gigawatt purchase
  pathways, Amazon dedicated capacity, and Microsoft/Azure relationship.
- Anthropic's AWS/Trainium commitment above $100 billion, Google/Broadcom TPU path,
  and terminable SpaceX Colossus capacity.
- xAI/SpaceX's Valor-financed NVIDIA equipment and common-control asset perimeter.
- Mistral Compute's announced GPU and megawatt targets.
- Cohere's CoreWeave-operated Cambridge tenancy.
- Broadcom's investor-owned rack structure and Google custom-TPU supply.

These records are leads, not a completed Chapter 3 ledger. Contract mechanics and
physical status must be re-extracted at the compute-unit level.

## First contradiction queue

1. OpenAI's NVIDIA ten-gigawatt letter of intent versus its later five-gigawatt
   dedicated capacity statement and AMD's separate six-gigawatt pathway.
2. Anthropic's approximately 325,000 Colossus GPU prospectus count versus the
   “more than 220,000” official partnership announcement.
3. RPO or announced contract value versus enforceable minimum spend at CoreWeave,
   Oracle, Microsoft, Amazon, Google, and Fluidstack.
4. GPU ownership versus use in CoreWeave, Colossus, Mistral Compute, and
   investor-owned Broadcom rack structures.
5. Power capacity in gigawatts versus deliverable IT load, GPU count, and accepted
   service capacity.

## Pass 1A — AMD purchase-linked warrants

The OpenAI and Meta arrangements share the same headline structure but are not
identical contracts.

| Term | OpenAI | Meta |
|---|---|---|
| Binding base purchase | 1 GW of MI450 products | 1 GW equivalent of specified Instinct products |
| First warrant trigger | delivery of initial 1 GW | shipment of initial 1 GW equivalent |
| Maximum purchase pathway | 6 GW | 6 GW equivalent |
| Maximum warrant | 160M shares at $0.01 | 160M shares at $0.01 |
| Expiry | 5 Oct. 2030 | 23 Feb. 2031, with limited regulatory extension |
| Vesting schedule | redacted | redacted |
| Exercise schedule | redacted | redacted |
| Tax characterization | sales discount or allowance | sales discount, allowance, or rebate |

The delivery-versus-shipment distinction means the first equity milestone may attach
at a different point in physical and acceptance performance. Public documents do not
define title passage, acceptance tests, payment timing, or whether the difference is
economically material.

The OpenAI warrant calls itself a material inducement to enter the supply agreement.
Both warrants link tax treatment to product consideration. This is stronger evidence
than a generic “strategic partnership”: customer equity upside is contractual purchase
consideration.

It is not possible to calculate the effective discount per GPU or per gigawatt. The
tranche sizes, product schedule, exercise conditions, and commercial milestones are
redacted. Treating all 320 million shares as a current rebate would ignore nonvesting,
stock-price conditions, AMD termination rights, and the fact that no shares had vested
at the latest filing date.

### Warrant failure path

Purchase or technical milestone missed  
→ tranche remains unvested  
→ AMD termination for cause or expiry cancels the tranche  
→ only then-exercisable shares automatically exercise  
→ customer equity benefit and AMD dilution fall together with product volume.

## Pass 1B — Colossus contract versus installed capacity

The SpaceX prospectus identifies three operational clusters:

| Cluster | Processor count | Compute power | Status |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Colossus H100 | approximately 100,000 | approximately 130 MW | operational |
| Colossus II GB200 | approximately 110,000 | approximately 210 MW | operational |
| Colossus II GB300 | approximately 110,000 | approximately 220 MW | operational |
| **Enumerated operational total** | **approximately 320,000** | **approximately 560 MW** | arithmetic |

The broader Colossus system is reported at approximately 1.0 GW, and a future Colossus
II phase is expected to add at least 220,000 GB300 processors and more than 400 MW.
The future phase is not included in operational inventory.

Anthropic's cloud agreements cover approximately 325,000 GPUs across Colossus I and II
at $1.25 billion per month after reduced May and June ramp pricing. Either party may
terminate after the initial three-month period on 90 days' notice.

The contract count exceeds the three specifically enumerated clusters by approximately
5,000 GPUs. That difference may be rounding, other installed capacity, model mix, or
contract allocation. It does not prove overcommitment. It does prove that the 325,000
contract count cannot be assigned one-for-one to the three named clusters.

SpaceX states that it designs, owns or leases, installs, and operates its powered shells
and dedicated processor capacity. Operator control is therefore clear, while individual
equipment title is mixed and remains unresolved because the Valor structures include
leased and financed assets.

## Pass 1C — NVIDIA/OpenAI: ten-gigawatt intent versus five-gigawatt expansion

The September 2025 joint announcement is a letter of intent rather than a filed
purchase contract. It describes at least ten gigawatts of NVIDIA systems and an
NVIDIA intention to invest as much as $100 billion progressively as each gigawatt is
deployed. The parties explicitly said they were finalizing definitive-agreement
details.

OpenAI's February 2026 financing announcement then describes an “expanding”
collaboration consisting of three gigawatts of dedicated inference and two gigawatts
of training on Vera Rubin. It does not say whether those five gigawatts:

- replace the earlier ten-gigawatt scope;
- are the first defined subset of it;
- sit inside it but on a different measurement perimeter; or
- are incremental to it.

The figures therefore share one overlap group and are not additive. The five-gigawatt
statement is stronger evidence of a specified deployment mix but still does not
publish minimum spend, delivery acceptance, title, operator allocation, remedies, or
definitive purchase agreements.

NVIDIA's Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 26 April 2026 provides a second accounting
boundary. It does not identify OpenAI or a ten-gigawatt customer contract. It reports
$27 billion of aggregate investment commitments subject to contingencies and expected
through the remainder of fiscal 2027. That aggregate cannot be assigned to OpenAI, and
it is not evidence that the headline “up to $100 billion” has become funded cash or a
present legally enforceable obligation. Conversely, the absence of a named OpenAI
commitment does not prove that no obligation exists.

### Status disposition

| Public figure | Instrument | Current treatment |
|---|---|---|
| At least 10 GW | letter of intent | nonadditive announced pathway |
| 3 GW inference + 2 GW training | later official expansion statement | specified but contract terms undisclosed |
| Up to $100B | progressive investment intent | not compute contract value and not present funding |
| $27B | NVIDIA aggregate contingent investment commitments | accounting boundary with no public counterparty allocation |

## Pass 1D — AWS cloud commitments: stated value is not yet minimum cash

### OpenAI

Amazon's first-quarter 2026 filing establishes a contractual relationship rather than
a press-release aspiration:

- an existing $38 billion multi-year AWS commitment;
- a $100 billion expansion over eight years;
- contractual obligations tied to performance of AWS chips; and
- an AWS/OpenAI commercial arrangement with an OpenAI affiliate.

The same note reports approximately $364 billion of aggregate customer performance
obligations primarily related to AWS and says revenue timing depends on customer usage
and Amazon's performance. It does not allocate the OpenAI portion of RPO. It also does
not disclose annual consumption, private pricing, service credits, minimum take,
termination payments, assignment, or remedies.

The correct status is therefore **contracted stated value, unresolved cash floor**.
Calling the entire $138 billion cancellable would understate the filing. Calling it an
unconditional minimum cash liability would add terms that the filing does not supply.

### Anthropic

Anthropic states that it committed more than $100 billion over ten years to AWS
technologies to secure up to five gigawatts. The scope includes Graviton and Trainium2
through Trainium4, with an option to purchase later chip generations. It reports:

- more than one million Trainium2 chips already in use;
- nearly one gigawatt of Trainium2 and Trainium3 expected by year-end 2026; and
- the remainder of the up-to-five-gigawatt path not physically delivered at
  announcement.

The financial and physical commitments are linked but not equivalent. “More than
$100 billion” is a lower bound on stated commercial value. “Up to five gigawatts” is
an upper bound on capacity. Neither statement discloses a unit price or proves that
the lower-bound dollars are an unconditional minimum-spend floor.

Amazon's filing supplies a separate transmission mechanism: a financing facility of
up to $20 billion initially had no drawable amount, and capacity becomes drawable as
Amazon reaches compute-delivery milestones. If delivery slips, two consequences can
travel together:

AWS capacity delivery misses  
→ Anthropic receives less usable compute  
→ less financing capacity becomes available  
→ Anthropic must fund growth elsewhere or slow expenditure.

This is a customer-financing loop linked to supplier performance, not evidence that
Amazon prepays Anthropic's AWS bill. The facility purchases Anthropic securities when
drawn.

### Microsoft/OpenAI

The October 2025 definitive agreement is the strongest of the three headline-value
records on the existence of a purchase contract: Microsoft states that OpenAI
contracted to purchase an incremental $250 billion of Azure services. In the same
agreement, Microsoft surrendered its right of first refusal to provide OpenAI compute.

That combination matters. The purchase commitment increased while Microsoft's
exclusive control over future compute sourcing decreased. It is therefore a large
contracted demand claim, not proof that Azure would provide all incremental OpenAI
capacity.

An April 2026 amendment then changed the relationship again:

- Microsoft remained the primary cloud partner;
- OpenAI products would ship first on Azure unless Microsoft could not and chose not
  to support the capabilities;
- OpenAI could serve all products across other clouds;
- Microsoft's OpenAI model and product IP license became nonexclusive;
- Microsoft stopped paying revenue share to OpenAI; and
- OpenAI's revenue share to Microsoft continued through 2030 subject to a cap.

The amendment announcement does not restate the $250 billion purchase or say whether
its economics changed. The ledger therefore preserves the October contract value but
marks the current terms as potentially amended. Public evidence still does not
disclose consumption timing, minimum take, Azure pricing, customer-specific Microsoft
RPO, service credits, termination payments, or the OpenAI obligor.

### Comparable status ladder

| Relationship | Public value | Contract evidence | Unavoidable-cash evidence |
|---|---:|---|---|
| OpenAI–Microsoft | $250B incremental | definitive agreement | unresolved and later terms amended |
| OpenAI–AWS | $38B existing + $100B expansion | SEC-described contractual commitment | unresolved; usage and AWS performance matter |
| Anthropic–AWS | more than $100B | signed commitment | unresolved; “up to” capacity and delivery-linked financing |

The three figures are not comparable measures of present liability. They differ in
term, delivery status, hardware perimeter, amendment history, and disclosed conditions.

## Pass 1E — CoreWeave: order value, remedies, and residual-value transfer

CoreWeave's named order values are upper-value disclosures, but the filed master
agreements reveal who carries the loss after failure.

### OpenAI order

The OpenAI order is disclosed at up to approximately $6.5 billion through 31 May 2031.
The May 2025 bare-metal MSA provides:

- termination for material breach after a redacted cure period and for insolvency;
- a transition period in which OpenAI pays only for services actually received;
- use of prepayments to set off amounts owed during transition;
- CoreWeave authority to sell or reconfigure customer-configured servers after
  CoreWeave terminates for customer breach;
- a CoreWeave best-efforts duty to mitigate by reselling those servers;
- resale proceeds credited against OpenAI's remaining amount; and
- an OpenAI right to terminate the affected order and recover unused prepayments after
  a force-majeure interruption lasting thirty consecutive days.

The order form outranks the MSA and material commercial fields are redacted. The
publicly visible mechanism nevertheless shows that the $6.5 billion ceiling is neither
cash already paid nor a pure walk-away option. Customer liability and supplier
residual-value recovery coexist.

### Meta order

Meta's initial order is disclosed at up to approximately $14.2 billion through
December 2031. Its filed MSA provides:

- if Meta terminates for CoreWeave breach, CoreWeave refunds prepaid fees for the
  unused term;
- if CoreWeave terminates for Meta breach, Meta owes remaining order-form fees;
- CoreWeave may sell or reconfigure reserved-instance servers;
- resale proceeds reduce Meta's amount owed;
- Meta has no right, title, or interest in CoreWeave systems;
- sufficiently long force majeure permits termination without cost and refund of
  prepayments; and
- a change of control to a direct competitor creates another termination path.

This allocates hardware residual value to CoreWeave and customer-credit exposure to
CoreWeave's contract and lenders. It also prevents treating Meta's cloud order as
ownership of the corresponding GPUs.

### NVIDIA residual-capacity order

NVIDIA's $6.3 billion initial-value order is structurally different. NVIDIA is obligated
to purchase capacity only when CoreWeave has residual unsold capacity after serving
its own customers and only when delivery and service-availability requirements are
satisfied. Either side may terminate for uncured breach after thirty days or specified
insolvency proceedings.

NVIDIA therefore occupies three positions at once:

GPU supplier  
→ CoreWeave equity holder  
→ conditional buyer of residual GPU-cloud capacity.

The order reduces some utilization risk, but it does not guarantee all capacity. It
also cannot be added to OpenAI and Meta order values as independent end demand without
testing whether the same physical capacity can move between those layers.

### Accounting boundary

CoreWeave's $98.8 billion RPO at 31 March 2026 is net of estimated service credits,
delivery delays, and capacity CoreWeave retains the right to resell. That accounting
netting is closer to expected contract revenue than gross named order ceilings, but it
is still not cash collateral or a guarantee of collection.

## Pass 1F — Oracle: cash prepayment, customer-supplied GPUs, and project capital

Oracle states that the prepaid and customer-supplied hardware portions of its large AI
contracts total $75 billion. That is not a $75 billion customer cash balance. It merges:

1. cash a customer prepaid to Oracle for Oracle to purchase GPUs; and
2. GPUs a customer bought and supplied to Oracle.

The two modes have different title, cash-flow, revenue-recognition, depreciation,
collateral, and residual-value consequences.

Oracle's audited fiscal-2026 cash-flow statement separately records a $4.592 billion
increase in deferred revenue from customer prepayments containing a significant
financing component. This proves that at least one cash-prepayment channel reached the
accounts. It does not split the $75 billion aggregate. It would be invalid to subtract
$4.592 billion and label the remainder customer-owned hardware because:

- the $75 billion covers an unnamed large-customer cohort;
- some prepayments may sit in other periods or accounting classifications;
- hardware values and timing are not disclosed; and
- the release does not reconcile the management measure to the audited balance sheet.

### Saline Township chain

Related Digital's announced greater-than-one-gigawatt Saline Township campus exposes a
four-layer compute chain:

Related / Blackstone sponsor equity + PIMCO-anchored long-term debt  
→ Related develops the $16 billion campus  
→ Oracle takes infrastructure capacity  
→ OpenAI supplies end demand through its Oracle compute relationship.

DTE supplies power and Oracle funds a new battery investment. The project announcement
does not disclose debt/equity amounts, lease payments, Oracle's cloud margin, OpenAI
minimum spend, collateral, maturity, recourse, or power price. The $16 billion project
cost therefore cannot be counted as an OpenAI compute obligation or Oracle capex.

### Risk transmission

OpenAI demand shortfall  
→ Oracle capacity utilization and RPO conversion pressure  
→ Oracle lease and cloud economics weaken  
→ project sponsor and lender coverage tightens  
→ physical assets remain with the project-owning layer.

The transmission is real even where OpenAI does not own the GPUs or data center. The
loss allocation depends on contracts that remain undisclosed.

## Pass 1G — Anthropic TPU scope and demand-contingent consumption

Broadcom's April 2026 Form 8-K identifies approximately 3.5 gigawatts of
next-generation TPU compute that Anthropic will access through Broadcom beginning in
2027. The filing also says:

- this capacity is part of multiple gigawatts committed by Anthropic;
- consumption depends on Anthropic's continued commercial success; and
- the parties were still discussing operational and financial partners.

The first clause is a capacity commitment. The second makes future consumption
economically contingent. The third shows that the physical and financing chain was not
fully closed at announcement.

Anthropic's May 2026 Series H announcement then describes signed agreements with Google
and Broadcom for five gigawatts of next-generation TPU capacity. It does not publish an
amendment or explain the change from 3.5 to 5 gigawatts.

The disciplined reconciliation is:

| Measure | Scope | Additive treatment |
|---|---|---|
| approximately 3.5 GW | specifically accessed through Broadcom from 2027 | included within broader path |
| 5 GW | later Google-and-Broadcom agreement scope | current upper scope; not added to 3.5 GW |

This is not evidence of 8.5 gigawatts. Nor is it proof that all five gigawatts will be
consumed: Broadcom tied consumption to Anthropic's commercial success.

### Transmission path

Anthropic revenue growth disappoints  
→ contracted TPU consumption may scale below planned capacity  
→ Google and Broadcom order economics weaken  
→ operational or financial partners retain underutilized assets or claims  
→ supplier backlog and project-finance coverage face pressure.

The undisclosed contract determines how much of that path is legal liability versus
economic exposure.

## Pass 1H — River Bend: guaranteed rent is not guaranteed delivery

River Bend is a completed credit structure around an uncompleted physical asset:

- Hut 8 signed a 15-year triple-net lease with Fluidstack for 245 MW of IT capacity;
- base-term rent totals $7.0 billion with a 3% annual escalator;
- Google backstops base rent and related pass-through obligations;
- the initial hall is scheduled for second-quarter 2027;
- Hut 8 DC issued $3.25 billion of 6.192% notes due 2042;
- substantially all project-issuer assets and issuer equity secure the notes; and
- the notes are ordinarily nonrecourse to Hut 8 Corp.

The structure substitutes Google credit for Fluidstack credit during operations, but
it does not make Google the tenant, Anthropic the bond obligor, or the data center
delivered. Construction and commissioning must occur before the rent stream behaves
like an operating asset.

### Optionality stripped from the headline

The “up to 2,295 MW” partnership is a three-part ladder:

| Tranche | Capacity | Legal/economic status |
|---|---:|---|
| River Bend base | 245 MW IT | executed landlord lease |
| River Bend expansion | up to 1,000 MW IT | Fluidstack right of first offer subject to power |
| Other Hut 8 sites | up to 1,050 MW | Hut 8 and Anthropic may jointly diligence and develop |

Only 245 MW belongs in contracted base capacity. The other 2,050 MW is optional or
prospective. Likewise, $17.7 billion assumes exercise of three five-year renewal
options and cannot replace the $7.0 billion base lease in a current obligation ledger.

### Control and loss path

Hut 8 DC owns project assets and owes bondholders  
→ Fluidstack owes rent under the lease  
→ Google backstops base rent and pass-through obligations  
→ Anthropic supplies model-company demand under an undisclosed compute relationship.

A construction failure first attacks delivery and project coverage. An Anthropic
demand failure may pressure the undisclosed Fluidstack relationship, but Google credit
can preserve landlord rent during the guaranteed base term. Guarantee protection
therefore moves rather than eliminates risk: Google absorbs tenant-payment exposure
while project completion remains with the development chain.

## Pass 1I — Anthropic on Azure: compute purchase and reciprocal capital

The November 2025 Microsoft/NVIDIA/Anthropic package has four separate legs:

1. Anthropic committed to purchase $30 billion of Azure compute;
2. Anthropic agreed to contract additional compute capacity up to one gigawatt;
3. that capacity initially uses NVIDIA Grace Blackwell and Vera Rubin systems; and
4. Microsoft and NVIDIA separately committed to invest up to $5 billion and
   $10 billion in Anthropic.

The $15 billion investment ceiling is not a discount against the $30 billion compute
purchase unless contract evidence says so. The one-gigawatt capacity also cannot be
added to the purchase value as dollars. Public language leaves unresolved whether the
one gigawatt is economically included in or incremental to the $30 billion purchase.

The package creates another reciprocal loop:

Microsoft and NVIDIA capital commitments  
→ Anthropic balance-sheet capacity  
→ Anthropic Azure purchase and NVIDIA-system demand  
→ Microsoft cloud revenue and NVIDIA hardware demand.

This does not make the arrangement circular accounting. It does mean that customer
credit quality, infrastructure utilization, and investor exposure are correlated.
Terms, funding conditions, delivery schedules, Microsoft RPO allocation, and remedies
remain undisclosed.

## Pass 1J — Asset finance: title migrates while risk survives

The hardware and facility finance pass produced a separate twenty-record
`compute_asset_finance` ledger. Its purpose is not to create another total. It records
which legal entity owns an asset, who operates it, who uses it, who finances it, what
has actually been funded or drawn, what is recognized, and which values overlap.

The structures fall into six distinct classes:

| Class | Representative case | Asset title | Principal risk carrier |
|---|---|---|---|
| project-SPV debt | CoreWeave CCAC VIII; River Bend | project subsidiary | secured lenders and project equity |
| vendor or OEM finance | CoreWeave equipment finance | operator subject to security interest | operator and equipment financier |
| operating or future lease | CoreWeave and Oracle campuses | third-party lessor | tenant after delivery; lessor before acceptance |
| customer-funded hardware | Oracle AI contracts | unresolved by aggregate disclosure | customer, Oracle, or both depending on contract |
| investor-owned rack lease | Broadcom structure | unnamed rack investor | customer, then Broadcom backstop |
| related-party equipment finance | SpaceX/xAI Valor transactions | accounting-dependent | consolidated SpaceX group plus related lessor |

### The figures cannot be added

The ledger deliberately marks nonadditive pairs:

- CoreWeave's $16.978 billion commenced lease payment schedule and $10.05 billion
  liability are undiscounted and present-value measures of the same portfolio.
- The single $18.7–$19.6 billion CoreWeave site may sit inside the separate
  $40.7 billion uncommenced-lease population.
- Oracle's $3.3 billion lessor-borrowing guarantee is embedded in its $260 billion
  uncommenced-lease population.
- River Bend's $3.25 billion funded notes and $7.0 billion rent schedule are debt
  principal and future contract revenue, not two measures of project cost.
- SpaceX's first two Valor payment schedules total $13.619 billion while their
  recognized failed-sale-leaseback debt was $9.041 billion. Both describe the same
  two transactions.
- Meta's $45.99 billion Hyperion maximum exposure already contains overlapping
  carrying value, future funding, lease, and guarantee components.

This is the central accounting constraint for Chapter 3: **contract value, asset cost,
funded cash, debt principal, lease payments, recognized liabilities, and contingent
maximum exposure are different measures even when they refer to the same compute.**

### Ownership does not identify the final loss bearer

Three cases demonstrate why:

1. Broadcom transfers rack title to an investor, the customer leases the racks, and
   Broadcom backstops up to $29 billion of customer lease obligations. Asset ownership
   moves away from Broadcom while contingent customer credit risk returns to it.
2. Hut 8 DC owns River Bend and bondholders have project collateral, Fluidstack owes
   rent, and Google backstops that rent. The asset, operating payment, and credit
   support sit at different nodes.
3. Alphabet's third-party infrastructure providers retain project or lease title, but
   Alphabet reports $37.4 billion of existing maximum credit support and can assume or
   sublease assets after certain defaults.

The chapter therefore cannot use “who owns the GPU” as a proxy for who absorbs loss.
Loss allocation requires the combined title, collateral, lease, guarantee, and
termination chain.

### Closed and unresolved points

The ledger resolves River Bend project ownership, debt funding, collateral, tenancy,
and backstop roles. It also distinguishes drawn CoreWeave project debt from total
commitment and records the accounting result of the first two Valor transactions.

The most material remaining title unknown is Oracle's $75 billion mixed population.
The disclosure combines customer cash with customer-purchased GPUs without identifying
the split, GPU-title terms, depreciation owner, or disposition rights. The separately
reported $4.592 billion fiscal-year increase in deferred revenue with a significant
financing component cannot be subtracted from $75 billion to solve that split.

## Pass 1K — Capacity-status waterfall and nonadditive reconciliation

The normalized waterfall contains 24 relationships. It counts cases, not gigawatts,
GPUs, megawatts, or dollars:

| Physical status | Relationship count |
|---|---:|
| fully delivered and documented | 4 |
| partially delivered | 5 |
| not delivered | 12 |
| unresolved | 2 |
| not a project-specific delivery claim | 1 |

The utilization-or-payment layer is thinner:

| Utilization/payment status | Relationship count |
|---|---:|
| documented operational | 3 |
| partial | 2 |
| not yet at that stage | 12 |
| unknown | 7 |

All 24 relationships have an announced scope. That does not imply that all 24 are
definitive contracts, financed projects, delivered capacity, or recognized revenue.
The waterfall retains “unknown” rather than treating missing disclosure as zero and
retains “partial” rather than estimating a completion percentage.

The delivered cases are the three enumerated Colossus clusters and the already
operational component of the Anthropic Colossus service relationship. The partial
population includes broad cloud relationships where some infrastructure exists but
the contracted expansion is not fully isolated, CoreWeave orders spanning current and
future service, and Mistral's partially operational internal platform. These
classifications do not make the cases economically comparable.

### Fifteen-case value reconciliation

A separate reconciliation aligns the most consequential dollar disclosures into four
columns:

1. headline commercial or project value;
2. funded or drawn capital;
3. recognized liability; and
4. contingent maximum exposure.

No total is reported because those columns measure different events. The reconstruction
instead identifies exactly why each row cannot be added or netted. Examples:

- $250 billion is a potentially amended Azure purchase value with no allocated customer
  RPO or cash draw.
- $75 billion at Oracle is a mixed customer-funding mechanism, while $4.592 billion is
  a fiscal-year deferred-revenue change with a financing component.
- $29 billion at Broadcom is a contingent lease backstop, while rack cost and customer
  rent are undisclosed.
- $16 billion at Saline is total project cost, not Oracle rent, PIMCO debt, or OpenAI
  cloud spend.

The practical result is that there is no defensible “total AI compute commitment”
formed by summing the public headlines. A useful system total would require a common
measurement basis and eliminations for overlapping service contracts, physical assets,
funding instruments, recognized liabilities, and guarantees. The evidence supports
relationship-level bounds, not a single industry liability number.

### Dependency graph

The frozen graph has 48 edges across compute purchases, project finance, title-to-
operator transitions, and operator-to-end-user transitions. Raw degree identifies
SpaceX, NVIDIA, Anthropic, OpenAI, CoreWeave, Oracle, Valor, and Hut 8 DC as frequent
nodes in this selected evidence set. This is a research-routing signal—not market
centrality—because disclosure depth and case selection affect edge count.
