# Chapter 9 Research Pass

## Provisional title

**Private Credit and Structured Finance — Where Leverage Leaves Public-Company
Accounts**

## Research question

How does AI-infrastructure credit move from a named data-center project into private
funds, warehouse facilities, securitizations, insurance balance sheets and
nontraded investment vehicles—and which contractual triggers can transmit economic
stress before a borrower misses an interest payment?

## Pass 1 scope

This chapter follows a financing claim through every disclosed legal and economic
layer:

1. project borrower and physical collateral;
2. sponsor equity and unfunded sponsor support;
3. originating lender, fund or managed account;
4. lender-level warehouse or subscription financing;
5. asset-backed securities and term-note distribution;
6. insurer, retirement, institutional and nontraded-fund capital;
7. valuation, borrowing-base, cash-trap and step-in controls;
8. share-repurchase or redemption mechanics at the capital-provider layer;
9. fee and related-party flows that continue before or during credit deterioration;
10. risk transmission from project delay or tenant weakness to marks, liquidity,
    financing capacity and ultimate capital providers.

Chapter 9 does not treat “private credit” as a single asset class and does not infer
an AI exposure merely because a manager originates infrastructure loans. It records
an exposure as AI or data-center-linked only when a borrower, collateral package,
tenant, facility or disclosed industry classification provides documentary support.

## Required reconstruction bridges

1. **Project-to-loan bridge:** physical project → borrower → loan facility →
   lender participation → funded balance and unfunded commitment.
2. **Loan-to-warehouse bridge:** eligible collateral → advance rate → borrowing
   base → cash account → lender remedies and servicer control.
3. **Lease-to-ABS bridge:** property → tenant lease → property owner / issuer →
   mortgage or ABS note → noteholder.
4. **Fund-capital bridge:** investor subscription or insurer liability → fund or
   general account → investment → fee and distribution → repurchase or redemption.
5. **Valuation bridge:** project performance → loan mark → collateral eligibility
   or advance ratio → fund NAV → investor liquidity.
6. **Loss-recognition bridge:** construction delay or tenant weakness → equity
   cushion → fair-value change → cash trap / funding need → nonaccrual → realized
   loss or default.

## Non-negotiable measurement rules

- A fund's total assets are not AI exposure.
- A manager's assets under management are not capital committed to a transaction.
- A financing “solution” is not necessarily funded debt, sponsor equity or project
  cost.
- A maximum facility size is not the amount drawn.
- A borrowing-base commitment is not additive to the loans that secure it.
- A lender's loan principal, cost and fair value are three different measures.
- An unfunded delayed-draw commitment is not a funded asset, but it is a future
  liquidity obligation.
- “First lien” does not establish low risk without loan-to-cost, collateral,
  completion, tenant and intercreditor terms.
- Level 3 classification means observable market inputs are limited; it does not
  mean the asset is impaired.
- No nonaccrual is not evidence that project economics remain unchanged.
- A quarterly share-repurchase limit is not a promise to provide liquidity.
- A distribution labeled return of capital is not automatically an economic loss,
  but it must not be described as operating income.
- Insurance and retirement liabilities may finance private assets; an AI-specific
  insurer exposure requires asset-level evidence.
- The same loan cannot be counted once in a fund, again as warehouse collateral and
  again in a project debt total when constructing a system-wide capital estimate.

## Pass 1A — Ares reveals the chain from project loan to bank warehouse

At 31 March 2026, Ares Core Infrastructure Fund reported six data-center loans with
an aggregate fair value of $405.076 million. Four were first-lien loans to CMH01
Holdings, CW Nest Property Owner, Galaxy Helios and PowerHouse Data LCX. Two were
senior subordinated loans to retained Vantage Data Centers and STACK SJ entities.
Their aggregate fair value represented 11.7% of the fund's investment fair value and
12.94% of net assets.

The funded balance is not the complete exposure. The same six loans carried
$301.583 million of unfunded commitments. That amount is effectively the fund's
entire $301.6 million portfolio of unfunded delayed-draw loans at quarter end. The
future funding obligation is therefore more concentrated in data centers than the
reported funded portfolio.

Every disclosed data-center position is valued with Level 3 inputs. The fund had no
investments on nonaccrual, but these observations measure different things:

`Level 3 valuation ≠ impairment ≠ nonaccrual ≠ default`

A useful natural experiment appears in the industry labels. At December 2025 the
same borrower set was divided between “IT services” and “specialized REITs.” At
March 2026 it appeared as “data centers,” with no transaction or balance-sheet event
required for the change. Industry classification can therefore conceal or reveal a
thematic concentration without changing the underlying contracts.

On 26 June 2026, the fund's ACI Liquid Aggregator SPV amended a facility with BNP
Paribas to create a data-center tranche capped at the lesser of $175 million and
asset-specific advance rates applied to eligible data-center loans. Each eligible
loan requires a bilateral data-center addendum. The published agreement exposes the
mechanism but not the most decision-useful calibration: borrower-specific advance
rates, event-of-default advance rates and the mapping from the six reported loans to
executed addenda are not public.

The agreement nevertheless documents a pre-default transmission pathway. A data-
center advance ratio above 125% creates a cure process. Following specified defaults,
the bank can control accounts, remove the servicer, direct dispositions and exercise
collateral remedies. A private loan can thus remain current while a lower mark,
eligibility change or funding obligation reduces the fund's warehouse capacity.

## Pass 1B — The Helios cross-check resolves one participation's physical project

Galaxy's filing identifies the larger loan behind Ares's $76.259 million fair-value
Galaxy Helios position. Galaxy Helios I has a senior secured term facility of up to
$1.4 billion from Deutsche Bank, with $1.1 billion drawn at 31 March 2026. It bears
interest at SOFR plus 4.75%, subject to a 2.5% SOFR floor, and matures in August
2028. Before stabilization it is subject to an 80% maximum loan-to-cost ratio; after
stabilization it is subject to a 1.40x minimum debt-service coverage ratio.

The collateral economics depend initially on one customer. The first Helios phase
supports CoreWeave, and the broader committed lease program is expected to deliver
526 megawatts of critical IT load. CoreWeave has contractual milestone and
termination rights. Ares's position is therefore a participation in a much larger
construction facility whose ultimate cash flow depends on delivery and one tenant's
performance—not a stand-alone diversified “data-center loan.”

Galaxy had $706.5 million of outstanding construction commitments for Helios at the
same reporting date. That commitment is not additive to the $1.1 billion drawn debt
as project cost; it identifies a separate future cash requirement whose timing and
financing bridge matter.

On 23 July 2026, Galaxy announced pricing of $3.507 billion of 9.875% senior secured
notes through Galaxy Helios Data Centers II to finance two additional buildings,
eight data halls and debt-service reserves. The announced closing date was 28 July,
after this chapter's research date. The amount is therefore recorded as
**priced / subject to closing**, not funded or drawn.

## Pass 1C — Blue Owl combines the landlord, ABS issuer and CMBS investor roles

Blue Owl Digital Infrastructure Trust is a nontraded, perpetual-life REIT rather
than a listed data-center operator. At 31 March 2026 it reported $4.119 billion of
assets, $3.245 billion of net real estate, $154.842 million of CMBS investments and
$1.820 billion of indebtedness. Its $1.820 billion fixed-rate ABS mortgage notes
carried rates from 5.00% to 5.90%, with a 5.39% weighted average.

This structure creates two different credit positions inside the same vehicle:

1. the trust owns data-center real estate and finances it with lease-backed,
   property-secured ABS; and
2. it separately owns a floating-rate CMBS investment with a $155.242 million face
   amount and $154.842 million fair value.

The mortgage notes are nonrecourse to the broader trust but cross-collateralized by
specified properties, leases and related contracts. In March 2026 a STACK issuer
placed $695 million of Series 2026-1 Class A-2 notes at 5.00%, using proceeds in part
to repay prior ABS. This is refinancing and liability management, not new project
capital in the full headline amount.

The trust's shares do not trade on an exchange and NAV relies on fair values without
a public market. Its repurchase plan is capped at 3% of aggregate NAV per quarter,
is not guaranteed and may be suspended. Asset duration and shareholder liquidity
therefore operate on different clocks.

The trust also exposes related-party channels. An affiliate manages properties for a
fee based on base rent, and a Blue Owl capital-markets affiliate can participate in
financings. Those fees are not evidence of misconduct; they are cash flows that must
be shown separately from property-level returns and noteholder economics.

## Pass 1D — Apollo's capital-solution headline is not Broadcom's backstop

Apollo announced that its funds and affiliates would lead an initial $35 billion
capital solution, with Blackstone and banks, for Broadcom's AI XPV platform. The
announcement says the platform is intended to support more than 20 gigawatts of
compute through 2028 and that the initial transaction facilitates more than one
gigawatt for Anthropic.

Broadcom's filed accounting disclosure describes a different measure: a contingent
customer-lease support obligation with maximum exposure of approximately $29
billion. The amounts cannot be added or substituted:

`$35B financing envelope ≠ $29B maximum Broadcom backstop`

The public record does not allocate the $35 billion among fund equity, bank debt,
asset-backed debt, insurer capital or funded asset purchases. It also does not
identify which Apollo or Blackstone funds bear the exposure. Until those instruments
and funded amounts are disclosed, the announcement establishes parties, purpose and
scale ambition—not a completed capital ledger.

## Pass 1E — Retirement liabilities are a plausible funding source, not a proven AI
allocation

Apollo combines an asset-management business with Athene's retirement-services
balance sheet. Athene reported $36.1 billion of investment commitments at 31 March
2026 and large balances of FHLB, funding-agreement-backed-note and other secured
funding agreements. It also disclosed material exposure to nonconsolidated
investment entities.

This proves the institutional pathway:

`retirement and funding-agreement liabilities → insurer general account →
Apollo-originated or managed assets`

It does not prove that a specified dollar of Athene capital funded Broadcom AI XPV,
Helios or another named AI project. No AI-specific allocation is made without an
asset-level filing, statutory schedule, transaction document or two-sided
counterparty disclosure.

## Pass 1F — Nontraded capital changes the timing of visible stress

Ares Core Infrastructure Fund and Blue Owl Digital Infrastructure Trust both offer
periodic, discretionary liquidity against portfolios containing illiquid assets.
Ares may repurchase up to 5% of shares per quarter; Blue Owl's limit is 3% of NAV.
These mechanisms are not equivalent to daily public-market liquidity.

Ares's first-quarter distribution exceeded the operating contribution reported in
its financial highlights and included a $0.6249-per-share return-of-capital
component. That label does not by itself establish deterioration. It does show why
cash distributions, net investment income, realized return and change in NAV must be
reconciled rather than treated as one return measure.

Private-asset stress can therefore appear in this order:

1. project milestone or cost assumptions worsen;
2. a Level 3 loan or property mark changes;
3. collateral eligibility or advance capacity contracts;
4. the vehicle needs cash for delayed draws, debt service or repurchases;
5. repurchases are reduced, prorated or suspended;
6. only later does a loan move to nonaccrual or default.

This sequence is a documentary mechanism, not a prediction that any named vehicle is
currently distressed.

## Contradictions and retained boundaries

- Apollo's $35 billion announcement and Broadcom's $29 billion maximum backstop are
  different instruments and measurement bases.
- Galaxy's July notes were priced but had not reached the announced closing date at
  the research cutoff.
- The Ares warehouse defines data-center collateral but the public agreement does
  not map executed addenda to the six portfolio loans.
- Ares's data-center classification changed between periods without an economic
  exposure change.
- “No nonaccrual” and “Level 3” answer different questions and are not contradictory.
- Athene's funding and origination perimeter is documented; its named AI-project
  exposure is not.
- Blue Owl's ABS debt and CMBS asset cannot be combined into a net “private-credit
  exposure” without identifying collateral and counterparties.
