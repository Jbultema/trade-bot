# Post-chapter synthesis deliverables

These artifacts are produced only after Chapters 1–19 pass their final publication
revision and the canonical database, claim registry, exposure registry, and chapter
manifests agree. They are generated from the investigation; they do not govern or
retrofit its conclusions.

Both synthesis artifacts must be delivered as separate professionally formatted
PDFs under `publication_pdf_standard.md`. Their source files, build manifests,
content hashes, and visual-inspection records remain separate from the complete
monograph publication.

## Executive summary

**Length:** 1–4 publication pages.

The summary must identify:

1. what the investigation reconstructed that cannot be read from any one company's
   reporting;
2. the largest and most decision-relevant capital, contract, control, dependency, and
   risk-transfer findings;
3. the most surprising findings and the prior intuitions they weaken;
4. which apparent circularities are documented, partial, merely overlapping, or
   unsupported;
5. where economic return is observed, bounded, scenario-dependent, or unresolvable;
6. the likely order in which stress becomes visible and the first-loss bearer where
   evidenced;
7. what remains materially unknowable;
8. the monitoring indicators most likely to change the conclusions; and
9. the confidence and evidence grade attached to every principal conclusion.

The summary must remain traceable to canonical claim and exposure identities. It may
compress the argument but may not omit a qualification that changes its economic
meaning.

## “Burning Up”-inspired essay for the current AI system

This is an original, publication-quality analytical article informed by the completed
forensic investigation. It is not a stylistic imitation, a generic bubble argument,
or a claim that the dot-com outcome must repeat.

The article must:

- state the present system's actual organizing mechanism;
- distinguish adoption success from producer, project, creditor, sponsor, and
  security-holder returns;
- identify what could “break up”: contracts, utilization assumptions, financing
  structures, project identities, cross-subsidies, governance alignments, or valuation
  narratives;
- show the likely causal sequence and its observed contractual bridges;
- distinguish an economic break from an accounting loss, refinancing event,
  operational impairment, or conventional default;
- identify first-loss and delayed-loss bearers only where the evidence supports them;
- compare the present system with the historical analogues using commensurate
  measures, including the negative controls;
- explain why healthy conventional credit metrics can coexist with deteriorating
  project economics;
- present the strongest resilience case as well as the strongest stress case;
- label Grade A/B facts, Grade C inference, and Grade D hypothesis at the point of
  use; and
- end with observable falsifiers and a monitoring framework rather than a theatrical
  prediction.

Every quantified statement must resolve to a canonical artifact or an explicitly
identified scenario. Apparent circularity must never be promoted into a closed cash
loop without transaction-level provenance.
