# Chapter 4 Research Pass

## Provisional title

**The Data-Center Financing Machine — Developers, SPVs, Lenders, Landlords, and Tenants**

## Research question

How does a proposed AI campus become a financed, constructed, commissioned, leased,
and cash-generating asset; which legal entity owns each stage; and who bears completion,
refinancing, utilization, residual-value, and default risk?

## Boundary from Chapters 2 and 3

Chapter 2 mapped entity-level capital and correlated counterparty pathways. Chapter 3
followed compute purchases and reservations through contract, finance, delivery, and
utilization. Chapter 4 follows the **physical project and its capital stack**.

It will not:

- treat a cloud purchase as project debt;
- treat undiscounted rent as construction cost;
- treat project cost as funded capital;
- treat a guarantee maximum as expected loss;
- treat an unconsolidated project as economically independent;
- treat a financed project as completed; or
- treat delivered shell capacity as accepted compute service.

## Required ledgers

1. **Project sources and uses** — sponsor equity, project debt, reimbursed development
   spend, construction budget, interest reserve, fees, equipment, power, and contingency.
2. **Ownership and control** — property owner, borrower, developer, operator, tenant,
   end user, guarantor, power counterparty, and consolidation conclusion.
3. **Construction and delivery** — land, permits, power, notice to proceed, contracted
   works, percent complete, commissioning, acceptance, and rent commencement.
4. **Collateral and recourse** — asset liens, equity pledges, contract assignments,
   reserves, completion support, carve-outs, guarantees, step-in, and cure rights.
5. **Revenue and coverage** — base rent, escalators, pass-throughs, service revenue,
   termination, renewal, minimum bills, resale rights, debt service, and coverage tests.
6. **Refinancing and maturity** — bridge, construction, term-out, bond, lease finance,
   maturity wall, rate, amortization, and takeout assumptions.
7. **Overlap and accounting bridge** — project cost versus debt principal, lease
   payments, recognized assets and liabilities, guarantee maximum, and parent exposure.
8. **Failure waterfall** — construction delay, cost overrun, power delay, tenant
   default, guarantor default, technology obsolescence, refinancing failure, and
   stranded asset.

## Pass 1A — River Bend: a construction bond built around a lease and guarantee

River Bend is the first complete project-finance template because its public indenture
exposes the borrower, collateral, project accounts, revenue waterfall, reserve, lease
termination cure, and noteholder remedy.

### Capital stack

Hut 8 DC issued $3.25 billion of 6.192% senior secured notes at par on April 30,
2026. Interest is paid semiannually beginning November 2026, principal amortization
begins May 2028, and final maturity is November 2042. The notes are fully amortizing.
Their tenor therefore avoids a construction-to-term refinancing cliff and broadly
matches the 15-year lease period after initial delivery.

Proceeds serve four purposes:

1. construct the 245 MW critical-IT campus and related substation;
2. reimburse part of Hut 8's prior project equity;
3. fund debt-service reserves; and
4. pay transaction fees and expenses.

The allocation among those uses is not public.

Hut 8 later described the financing as approximately 95% loan-to-cost, replacing the
approximately 85% bank structure contemplated at lease signing. It said the project
was fully funded, $184 million of prior equity would return to Hut 8 at closing, and no
incremental Hut 8 equity was expected.

The public numbers do not form exact sources and uses:

| Reconstruction | Amount |
|---|---:|
| funded note principal | $3.250B |
| implied cost at exactly 95% LTC | $3.421B |
| implied non-debt cost at exactly 95% LTC | $0.171B |
| reported prior equity returned | $0.184B |

The $13 million difference is not an error to be allocated mechanically. “Approximately
95%” can be rounded, eligible project cost can differ from total uses, and reserves,
fees, reimbursement timing, or retained sponsor capital can alter the basis. Until an
offering budget or issuer financial statement appears, $3.421 billion is a bounded
arithmetic estimate rather than disclosed project cost.

### Revenue stack

Fluidstack's 15-year triple-net, take-or-pay base lease has $7.0 billion of stated
contract value and a 3% annual base-rent escalator. Hut 8 forecasts $6.9 billion of
cumulative NOI and $454 million of average annual NOI. Google backstops base rent and
pass-through obligations, and the parties expected a separately Google-supported
operations-services agreement.

The average NOI is a management forecast, not current revenue. Initial delivery is
scheduled for second-quarter 2027. Construction, commissioning, acceptance, and lease
commencement sit between funded debt and operating cash flow.

### Collateral and accounts

The notes have first-priority liens on substantially all issuer assets, controlled
project accounts, and a pledge of the equity in Hut 8 DC. They are nonrecourse to Hut 8
Corp., but “nonrecourse” does not mean covenant-free. The issuer is restricted from
unrelated operations, additional debt, liens, asset sales, affiliate transactions,
material lease or guarantee amendments, and several organizational changes.

During construction, the debt-service-reserve formula starts with $293.475 million,
representing the next three scheduled interest payments less interest already paid,
and also includes half of the next interest payment and half of the first principal
installment. The first installment amount is not public.

### Operating cash waterfall

After commencement, project cash follows this priority:

1. operating expenses;
2. current note debt service;
3. debt-service-reserve replenishment; and
4. other permitted uses.

Utility, energy, and specified third-party pass-through cash can bypass the controlled
revenue account. Residual cash is therefore distributable after senior requirements,
subject to coverage and restricted-payment tests; it is not permanently trapped.

### Tenant-loss waterfall

If the Fluidstack lease terminates, the issuer has six months to enter a qualifying
replacement lease. The period can extend to twelve months if it has an LOI or similar
agreement with Google or another qualifying tenant, a fully required reserve,
operating and maintenance liquidity, restricted investment behavior, and any received
termination fee preserved in the designated account.

Any termination fee payable by Fluidstack or Google funds a par note-repurchase offer.
Failure to replace the lease and then make the required offer escalates into an event
of default. The indenture also restricts material adverse changes to the Fluidstack
lease or Google guarantee without noteholder consent or rating-agency confirmation.

This yields a more precise failure chain than “Google guarantees the project”:

Fluidstack lease ends  
→ Google or Fluidstack termination fee may become payable under private documents  
→ issuer searches for a qualifying replacement tenant  
→ reserve and maintenance liquidity bridge six to twelve months  
→ received termination fee funds a par note-repurchase offer  
→ only failure to complete the defined remedy escalates to the lease-termination event
of default.

The private lease and Google guarantee still determine the amount and trigger of the
termination fee. The indenture routes that cash but does not disclose its maximum.

## Pass 1B — Saline Township: one campus and a separate utility asset pool

The Saline evidence resolves a critical legal-entity question while making the financing
reconstruction more—not less—complex. Related's announcement described a $16 billion
Oracle campus funded with Related and Blackstone equity and PIMCO-anchored fixed-rate
debt. The Michigan utility record identifies a different entity and a second capital
stack: Green Chile Ventures LLC, an Oracle subsidiary, is DTE's customer and plans to
lease, operate, cause construction of, and commission the facility. OpenAI is downstream
compute demand. It is not a party to the filed utility contracts.

### The five-node contractual chain

| Node | Documented role | What is not established |
|---|---|---|
| Related / Blackstone | developer and sponsor-equity sources | exact contributions and ownership percentages |
| Saline project entity | campus owner and project borrower placeholder | exact legal name, debt amount, collateral, and recourse |
| Green Chile Ventures | Oracle subsidiary, facility lessee/operator, and DTE customer | campus rent and landlord remedies |
| Oracle | Green Chile parent and utility-credit support source | campus-lease guarantee and utility-support cap |
| OpenAI | stated end demand under Oracle's compute partnership | direct campus, utility, or project-finance obligation |

This chain changes the risk map. A failure of OpenAI consumption must first propagate
through Oracle's cloud economics and Green Chile before it reaches campus rent or
regulated utility payments. The documentary record does not permit the $16 billion
project cost to be described as OpenAI capital expenditure or an OpenAI contract value.

### The primary supply contract

The Commission conditionally approved a Primary Supply Agreement under which Green
Chile receives service on DTE's D11 tariff through February 28, 2045. Green Chile has
two optional ten-year renewals. The contract applies an 80% minimum billing demand to
the committed load ramp, which reaches 1,383 MW. Green Chile can defer the ramp by no
more than twelve months in aggregate—moving maximum load from December 2027 to no later
than December 2028—but cannot reduce the maximum load DTE has committed to serve.

The exit payment is capacity-based rather than a disclosed fixed dollar amount. Before
the tenth anniversary of load-ramp completion it equals minimum billing demand times
the greater of:

1. months remaining to that tenth anniversary; or
2. twenty-four months.

After the tenth anniversary, it equals twenty-four months of minimum monthly charges.
The PSA and Energy Storage Agreement also use a parent guaranty and, if the parent's
credit rating requires it, a letter of credit. The public versions do not disclose
support caps or collateral dollars.

### A 1,383 MW utility-owned asset pool

The Energy Storage Agreement is not merely an obligation for Oracle to “finance a
battery.” It creates a portfolio as large as the data center's maximum load. DTE can
build, acquire through build-transfer arrangements, or contract for up to 1,383 MW of
storage. Each DTE-owned project has a fifteen-year revenue requirement beginning at
commercial operation and including:

- allowance for funds used during construction;
- depreciation;
- DTE's authorized return;
- investment-tax-credit amortization;
- operating and maintenance expense;
- property tax; and
- insurance.

Third-party tolling projects pass through their annual contract cost plus any authorized
incentive. DTE reconciles estimated and actual costs annually. Green Chile receives
incremental MISO capacity, energy, and ancillary-service revenues net of market and
charging costs as a monthly bill credit, while DTE charges an administrative fee for
market participation.

Green Chile cannot voluntarily terminate the storage agreement. A default termination
payment equals the unrecovered portion of every project's fifteen-year revenue
requirement. The first 332 MW of this planned 1,383 MW portfolio had obtained Commission
approval by March 2026. “Approved” does not mean constructed, commissioned, or
operational.

### The announcement's power statement required correction

Related said DTE would supply the project using existing resources augmented by a
battery financed by Oracle. DTE's sworn testimony is more precise: existing resources
were insufficient in all seasons to meet the higher planning-reserve requirement, and
incremental storage was needed for resource adequacy.

The statements are not necessarily mutually exclusive about energy production—existing
generation can supply energy while storage adds capacity adequacy—but the announcement
omitted the scale and separate ownership of the required resource portfolio. The
research ledger therefore supersedes the earlier simplified claim.

### The residual risk did not disappear

The contracts contain strong ratepayer protections, but they do not eliminate loss.
The Commission conditioned approval on Green Chile's payments covering generation,
transmission, distribution, and other service costs. If Green Chile, the parent
guaranty, and the letter of credit do not cover the remaining PSA or ESA exposure,
DTE—not other ratepayers—must bear the residual liability. The Commission also ordered
the data center curtailed before other firm customers when doing so can prevent broader
emergency load shedding.

The resulting loss sequence is:

1. Green Chile pays D11 charges and storage revenue requirements;
2. Oracle support and any letter of credit respond to defined defaults;
3. termination formulas accelerate a minimum amount or remaining storage requirement;
4. DTE applies recoveries and the value of storage retained for customers; and
5. DTE equity bears insufficiency under the Commission order.

This is a concrete example of risk migration. The storage assets sit on a regulated
utility's balance sheet and enter rate base, Green Chile contract revenue offsets their
cost, Oracle supports the customer, and regulatory policy assigns ultimate collateral
sufficiency risk to DTE.

### Nonadditive reconstruction

The two published capital pools must remain separate:

| Capital pool | Disclosed measurement | Owner / obligor perimeter |
|---|---:|---|
| Saline data-center campus | $16.0B announced project cost | Related / Blackstone project perimeter; exact borrower unresolved |
| DTE storage portfolio | up to 1,383 MW; cost undisclosed | DTE-owned or procured assets paid through Green Chile revenue requirements |

Adding an unknown storage cost to $16 billion would be speculative. Treating the
storage portfolio as already included in $16 billion would be equally speculative.
The definitive campus budget, project loan documents, Oracle lease, individual storage
orders, and DTE rate cases are the next-source path.

## Pass 1C — Hyperion: sell down ownership while retaining demand and tail risk

Hyperion is not a conventional sale of a completed data center and not a simple
construction loan. It is a venture formation in which Meta transferred partially built
assets, received cash back, retained minority equity, committed future funding, became
the future tenant and manager, and supplied terminal-value protection. Blue Owl's
affiliate became the 80% owner and brought cash plus a private-debt channel.

### Opening transaction

Meta's September 2025 quarterly filing provides the exact closing flows:

| Closing flow | Amount | Measurement |
|---|---:|---|
| Meta contribution | $4.30B | net assets consisting mostly of construction in progress and land |
| Blue Owl investor contribution | $7.00B | cash |
| distribution from venture to Meta | $(2.60B) | cash returned to Meta |
| net cash and assets retained before later activity | $8.70B | arithmetic reconstruction |

The opening net capital does not fund the full estimated $27 billion project. Comparing
the $8.7 billion reconstructed opening balance with estimated development cost leaves an
approximately $18.3 billion development-capital requirement. That gap can be filled by
future partner contributions, the disclosed private bond channel, or changes in cost.
It is not a disclosed loan balance. The contributed assets were net of liabilities, the
project budget is approximate, and the distribution's legal funding source is not
separately stated, so this is a bridge—not audited sources and uses.

The parties agreed to fund their pro-rata shares of estimated development cost. At a
20/80 ownership split, a purely arithmetic allocation of $27 billion is $5.4 billion to
Meta and $21.6 billion to the Blue Owl investor. Those are contractual-share estimates,
not a statement that each party supplies only equity: Meta's contribution and
distribution change its capital-account bridge, and transaction counsel says private
debt finances a portion of Blue Owl's capital.

### Revenue and support stack

Meta's leases begin in 2029. The initial four-year property leases total $12.31 billion,
with renewal options that can extend each property to twenty years. Optional rent is not
included in the disclosed initial commitment.

For the first sixteen years of operations, a residual-value guarantee protects the
venture if Meta terminates or does not renew a property lease and additional conditions
are satisfied. The threshold starts around $28 billion and declines over time. The
payment is not $28 billion automatically:

> eligible payment = maximum of zero and applicable threshold less property fair value

Meta concluded payment was not probable and recorded no guarantee liability through
March 2026. The public record does not expose the threshold by property and year, the
appraisal standard, guarantee caps, cure rights, or lender control of proceeds.

### The $45.99 billion maximum exposure is a stack of overlapping measures

At March 31, 2026, Meta reported:

| Meta measure | Amount |
|---|---:|
| venture investment carrying value | $2.37B |
| initial lease commitment | $12.31B |
| maximum residual-value threshold | approximately $28.00B |
| estimated future funding commitment | not stated separately |
| maximum exposure to loss | $45.99B |

Subtracting the three published inputs from maximum exposure implies approximately
$3.31 billion of future funding. That residual is directionally useful but not exact:
the guarantee threshold is rounded, maximum exposure may use property-level values,
and the filing does not state that every component uses the same date or precision.

The reconciliation matters because four different numbers answer four different
questions:

- $27 billion is estimated development cost;
- $12.31 billion is initial future rent;
- approximately $28 billion is a declining contingent threshold;
- $45.99 billion is Meta's accounting maximum exposure across investment, funding,
  rent, and contingent support.

They cannot be summed. Nor can $45.99 billion be called debt, funded capital, expected
loss, or Meta capital expenditure.

### Accounting control is not economic independence

Meta does not consolidate the venture because decisions about remarketing the campus,
negotiating future tenants, and selling individual properties most significantly affect
economic performance, and Meta lacks power over those decisions. Yet Meta remains:

- 20% investor;
- construction manager;
- administrative and property manager;
- future tenant of all campus facilities;
- pro-rata future funder; and
- residual-value guarantor.

Blue Owl-side control creates accounting separation. Meta's demand, management,
funding, rent, and guarantee create economic concentration. A project can therefore
sit outside a hyperscaler's consolidated balance sheet while remaining highly dependent
on that hyperscaler's continued credit and strategic use.

### Debt remains the principal documentary gap

Meta and transaction counsel state that debt issued to PIMCO and selected bond
investors finances part of the capital raised by Blue Owl-managed funds. No reviewed
public source supplies issuer, principal, coupon, maturity, amortization, collateral,
completion support, coverage tests, or investor allocations. The $27 billion development
estimate must remain separate from project debt until private-placement, mortgage, UCC,
rating, or fund evidence resolves the instrument.

The failure waterfall can nevertheless be bounded:

1. partner contributions and project debt fund construction;
2. Meta rent services operating costs, financing, and investor distributions after 2029;
3. on nonrenewal, the venture uses remarketing or property-sale proceeds;
4. when qualifying fair value is below the threshold, Meta pays the contractual
   residual-value shortfall; and
5. remaining loss falls through the undisclosed creditor priority into Blue Owl and
   Meta venture capital.

## Pass 1D — CCAC VIII: customer cash under lender control

CoreWeave's DDTL 4.0 is the clearest public example of GPU-backed project finance that
behaves like an infrastructure loan rather than general corporate debt. The customer,
sites, order forms, and several performance metrics are redacted, but the filed credit
agreement exposes how contract acceptance, GPU asset value, power cost, and cash
distribution interact.

### Commitment is not funding

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---:|
| total delayed-draw commitments | $8.50B |
| initial borrowing capacity before stabilization | approximately $7.50B |
| funded principal at March 31, 2026 | $1.26B |
| undrawn commitment at that date | approximately $7.24B |

The facility remains drawable through June 30, 2027 and matures March 31, 2032.
Approximately $4.5 billion is floating-rate capacity at compounded SOFR plus 2.25%,
and approximately $4.0 billion is fixed-rate capacity at a Treasury-derived yield plus
2.00%. Undrawn commitments cost 0.50% annually. The fixed rate for later drawings is
therefore not frozen at the roughly 5.9% initial rate highlighted by management.

Debt can fund GPU infrastructure acquisition and installation, a liquidity reserve, a
power reserve, transaction expense, and qualifying top-up distributions after assets
stabilize. Draws must remain within eligible equipment purchase-price and project
conditions. Debt availability is capacity subject to documentary and operating gates,
not unconditional cash.

### Asset acceptance determines leverage

The agreement sizes debt to a projected debt-service coverage ratio of at least 1.20x.
The model explicitly adjusts for:

- percentage of GPU clusters accepted by the customer;
- actual redacted service-performance measures;
- delay discounts and corresponding customer payments;
- actual or expected interest hedges;
- power and site costs; and
- whether a site is stabilized or delayed.

A delayed site with projected negative NOI can require enough repayment to eliminate
the negative month before it occurs, while the remaining portfolio must still project
at least 1.20x coverage. After the draw period, trailing three-month coverage cannot
fall below 1.15x.

This reveals the economic chain beneath the investment-grade label:

> customer acceptance → recognized service cash → site NOI → debt-sizing capacity →
> draw availability and distributions

GPU collateral is necessary but not sufficient. Lenders advance against the interaction
of depreciable equipment value and customer-supported cash flow.

### Collateral and parent recourse

Lenders hold security over substantially all CCAC VIII and subsidiary assets and 100%
of pledged borrower equity. Borrower subsidiaries guarantee the facility. CoreWeave
does not guarantee ordinary debt repayment. Its filed parent guarantee covers defined
bad acts:

- actual fraud;
- misappropriation of collateral or proceeds;
- willful misconduct causing material physical damage or waste; and
- voluntary bankruptcy or consent to specified insolvency actions.

The document explicitly says CoreWeave is not required to inject equity merely to
prevent damage when the project generates insufficient net funds. “Nonrecourse” is
therefore substantively meaningful here, but it remains subject to behavior-based
recourse and the group's operational role.

### The ten-line project waterfall

Although the contract calls its operating sequence nine priorities, separating inflow
from uses produces ten ledger lines:

1. customer and project cash enters the Available Cash Account;
2. operating expenses due or expected within sixty days;
3. agent, legal, rating, depositary, indemnity, and secured-swap fees;
4. scheduled interest and ordinary swap payments;
5. scheduled principal and swap termination amounts;
6. three-month liquidity-reserve replenishment;
7. excess-power-cost reserve replenishment;
8. cash trapping during a qualifying customer-contract breach or parent bankruptcy;
9. management agreement and other subordinate operating or capital costs; and
10. distribution reserve, intercompany debt, or other permitted residual use.

The liquidity reserve covers three months of principal, interest, swaps, and operating
expense. The power reserve covers power costs above the base-case model. Both can be
consumed by shortfalls and must be replenished before distributions.

### Customer concentration is embedded in the covenant design

A material breach of the redacted master-services agreement that gives either party
termination rights triggers a cash trap. A CoreWeave parent bankruptcy does the same.
That trigger ranks ahead of ordinary affiliate management costs and all residual
distributions. The facility is therefore legally isolated from ordinary parent debt,
but not informationally independent of customer performance or parent survival.

The customer identity remains unresolved. It would be methodologically invalid to infer
the name from CoreWeave's public customer relationships. Until the order forms or rating
materials identify it, the ledger uses a redacted-customer node and treats customer
concentration as known but unallocated.

## Pass 1E — Broadcom and Apollo: title transfer with a vendor tail

Broadcom's May 2026 quarter filing supplies an unusually compact example of AI
vendor financing. Note 11 describes an “investor partner,” but Item 5 names Apollo.
The transaction is therefore no longer an anonymous-investor case. The specific
Apollo fund, transaction SPV, and customer remain unidentified.

### What moved and what did not

Apollo agreed to take on certain agreements to purchase Broadcom-designed custom AI
accelerator racks and the related five-year customer leases. This moves initial rack
title and the direct lease receivable to an Apollo-side structure. It does not eliminate
Broadcom's customer-performance exposure:

- Broadcom's backstop increases as racks are deployed;
- it decreases as the customer makes lease payments;
- it is capped at $29 billion; and
- after customer default, Broadcom may assume the lease or cause the racks to be sold.

The $29 billion is a maximum contingent exposure. It is not disclosed rack cost,
Apollo debt, Apollo equity, customer lease value, present value, Broadcom recognized
liability, or expected loss. It is also not additive to Broadcom's $164.6 billion
remaining performance obligations or $128.11 billion purchase commitments.

### Reconstructed transaction sequence

1. an Apollo-side vehicle funds or assumes rack-purchase agreements;
2. custom accelerator racks are deployed and accepted;
3. the unidentified customer makes five-year lease payments to the Apollo lessor;
4. each deployment increases Broadcom's contingent ceiling usage while each payment
   reduces it;
5. customer default activates the vendor-support channel; and
6. Broadcom may assume the lease or cause a rack sale, with either recovery reducing
   the exposure.

No reviewed disclosure identifies acquisition cost, funding mix, collateral, reserve
accounts, payment priority, residual-value curve, cure rights, or which party bears
first loss before the Broadcom backstop. Those omissions prevent a conventional
sources-and-uses reconciliation. The project ledger consequently leaves all capital
amounts blank rather than reverse-engineering them from the guarantee ceiling.

### Economic result

Legal title and initial funding migrate away from the semiconductor vendor, but a
large tail remains linked to the same customer's payment performance. Broadcom's own
risk factors provide the motive: customers increasingly seek leased or fully financed
AI systems because of capital constraints, and these structures can reduce Broadcom
revenue, free cash flow, or margins and expose it to customer default.

This is not evidence that the transaction will lose money. It is evidence that the
system separates four roles that a headline can obscure:

| Role | Known party | Unresolved element |
|---|---|---|
| rack designer and vendor supporter | Broadcom | rack cost and support pricing |
| capital provider / titled owner | Apollo-side structure | fund, SPV, debt, and equity |
| rack operator and lease obligor | unidentified customer | identity, credit, and acceptance |
| residual recovery channel | Broadcom and Apollo structure | priority and equipment value |

The structure therefore belongs in Chapter 4 even without a named data-center site:
it finances the compute layer through an asset owner and lease contract while preserving
vendor step-in and disposition rights.

## Pass 1F — Cross-case reconstruction

The frozen comparison contains five Tier 1 cases, 21 delivery milestones, 41 cash or
failure-waterfall stages, five control and consolidation determinations, and six Tier 2
controls. Blank capital fields mean unresolved—not zero.

### Discovery 1: the customer contract is part of the collateral

In River Bend, contracted rent and a hyperscaler backstop support a fixed-rate project
bond. In CCAC VIII, customer acceptance and realized service performance determine how
much debt can be drawn and whether cash is trapped. In Hyperion, future Meta rent and a
conditional residual-value guarantee support capital outside Meta's consolidated
balance sheet. In Broadcom–Apollo, five-year lease payments directly run down the
vendor's contingent exposure.

The common financing asset is therefore not “the data center” or “the GPUs” alone:

> titled physical asset + accepted service obligation + controlled cash + recovery rights

The weighting differs by case. River Bend emphasizes contractual cash, CCAC VIII mixes
cash and movable GPU collateral, Hyperion emphasizes tenant credit and terminal value,
and Broadcom–Apollo leaves the capital structure private while exposing vendor recovery.

### Discovery 2: nonrecourse is a spectrum

| Case | Legal separation | Retained economic channel |
|---|---|---|
| River Bend | notes nonrecourse to Hut 8 Corp. | wholly owned issuer, construction and operating execution |
| Saline | project borrower and recourse undisclosed | Oracle utility support, sponsor capital, DTE residual recovery |
| Hyperion | unconsolidated VIE | Meta funding, rent, management, and residual-value guarantee |
| CCAC VIII | ordinary debt nonrecourse to CoreWeave | bad-act guarantee, platform operations, customer contract |
| Broadcom–Apollo | racks and leases outside Broadcom title | $29B maximum customer-lease backstop and step-in rights |

Legal nonrecourse answers who owes ordinary debt service. It does not answer who must
keep the project on schedule, supply demand, replenish reserves, protect terminal value,
or preserve franchise credibility.

### Discovery 3: the cleanest maturity match is also the most transparent structure

River Bend funds construction with fully amortizing debt due in 2042 against a
fifteen-year lease expected to begin in 2027. That avoids a planned construction-loan
takeout. CCAC VIII discloses a 2032 maturity and 2027 draw deadline but redacts the
customer schedule and principal model. Hyperion discloses a 2029 rent start but not the
private project bond's maturity. Saline discloses long utility obligations but no campus
loan maturity. Broadcom discloses five-year customer leases but no Apollo funding tenor.

This is an evidence result, not proof that the most transparent project is safest.
Private debt can be conservatively structured. It means public observers cannot test
asset life, contract tenor, amortization, and refinancing alignment for four of five
cases with the same precision available at River Bend.

### Discovery 4: power can create a parallel capital stack

Saline demonstrates why the data-center budget cannot stand in for total system capital.
The announced $16 billion campus sits beside a utility-owned or procured storage
portfolio of up to 1,383 MW. The storage cost is recovered under its own fifteen-year
revenue requirements and default formula. The campus and storage are contractually
linked but financially nonadditive absent a definitive budget proving inclusion.

This pattern changes risk transmission. A campus default can impair not only project
debt and sponsor equity but also a regulated utility asset pool, a parent guaranty, a
letter of credit, and regulatory cost-allocation rules.

### Discovery 5: title transfer often preserves the strongest counterparty's tail

Three structures move title or accounting presentation away from the operating sponsor:

- Hyperion places the campus in an unconsolidated Blue Owl-controlled venture while
  Meta remains tenant, funder, manager, and residual-value guarantor;
- Broadcom transfers rack purchase and lease agreements to Apollo while retaining a
  customer backstop and recovery rights; and
- Alphabet's Tier 2 infrastructure structures leave project title with providers while
  Alphabet supplies up to tens of billions of dollars of credit support and can obtain
  leases or assets after default.

The repeated design is not risk disappearance. It is risk partition:

1. construction and asset title move to a project or investor vehicle;
2. operating use stays with a customer or platform;
3. senior capital receives controlled cash and collateral;
4. terminal or default risk is partly returned to the strongest strategic balance sheet.

### Discovery 6: the measurement error is systematic

Each case exposes a different headline-measure trap:

| Headline | What it is | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| River Bend $3.25B | funded note principal | exact total project cost |
| Saline $16B | announced campus project cost | Oracle rent, OpenAI commitment, or PIMCO debt |
| Hyperion $45.99B | overlapping Meta maximum exposure | project debt or additive capital |
| CCAC VIII $8.5B | conditional delayed-draw commitment | funded balance or GPU purchase cost |
| Broadcom $29B | dynamic contingent backstop ceiling | Apollo financing, rack cost, or lease value |

The Tier 2 controls reproduce the same problem. Oracle's $3.3 billion lessor-borrowing
guarantee sits inside a $260 billion uncommenced-lease population. SpaceX/xAI's
$9.041 billion of recognized failed-sale-leaseback debt overlaps $20.206 billion of
undiscounted equipment payments and guarantees. Alphabet's later execution is drawn
against—not added to—its future backstop framework.

### Frozen project-level failure sequence

Across cases, the common sequence is:

1. sponsor, lender, investor, or vendor capital funds land, shell, power, or compute;
2. construction and installation proceed before revenue acceptance;
3. power, commissioning, and customer acceptance convert the asset into billable service;
4. customer cash enters a landlord, lessor, or controlled project account;
5. operating costs, secured fees, debt service, and reserves rank before equity;
6. customer or project distress traps cash and activates cure or replacement rights;
7. guarantees, lease assumption, remarketing, or asset sale address the shortfall; and
8. only then does residual loss reach lenders, investors, vendors, utilities, or parents
   according to documents that are often private.

The major hidden variable is not simply default probability. It is the recovery function:
the value and liquidity of a partly completed campus, customer-specific power build,
accelerator rack, or unexpired lease after the original demand chain breaks.
